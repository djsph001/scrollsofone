Scene: The Intern Disappears

She didn’t sleep.

She lay on her back in a room that felt suddenly too small, listening to the building creak like it was talking about her.

The USB was gone—hidden where she’d hidden things as a kid: inside a hollowed book spine, taped shut, slid behind a row of other books so it looked like nothing.

She kept replaying the moment her hand touched the console.

Copy.
Eject.
Hide.

It had felt like nothing.

Now it felt like a siren that only she could hear.

At 6:04 AM, her phone lit up.

Unknown number.

No text. Just an image.

A still frame of her face from the studio camera—sharp, zoomed, time-stamped.

Beneath it, one sentence:

DON’T DO THAT AGAIN.

Her stomach dropped.

She stared at it until her eyes burned.

Then her phone buzzed again—this time a calendar notification she didn’t recognize:

Meeting — 8:30 AM — Compliance Review

No location. No details.

Just certainty.

She sat up slowly, heart loud.

In the bathroom mirror, she looked like someone who’d been crying without tears.

She brushed her teeth anyway, hands shaking.

At 7:50 she left her apartment.

Not because she thought she could hide—
because if she didn’t show up, they’d come anyway.

Outside, the air was too cold. The street too normal.

A black sedan idled across from her building.

No flashing lights. No badges.

Just a car that knew where she lived.

She walked.

The sedan rolled behind her at a respectful distance, like a shadow pretending to be polite.

At 8:12, she turned a corner and the sedan turned too.

At 8:20, a second car appeared ahead of her—parked, engine running.

Her throat tightened.

Two cars was a message:

We don’t need to chase you.
We’ve already decided.

At 8:27, her phone buzzed again.

A text:

TURN LEFT.

She froze mid-step.

People flowed around her—commuters, headphones, coffee cups.

No one noticed.

That was the point.

She turned left.

At 8:29, another text:

DO NOT RUN.

Her knees felt watery.

She walked.

At 8:31, she reached a door she’d never noticed before—plain, industrial, unmarked.

The first car pulled to the curb.

A man got out—no uniform, no expression—opened the door and gestured politely as if she’d been invited.

“Morning,” he said.

Her voice came out thin.

“Am I in trouble?”

The man smiled like a human resources rep.

“You’re in review,” he said.

She wanted to scream.

Instead she stepped inside.

The door closed behind her with a sound that was too soft to be mercy.

Down a hallway, fluorescent lights hummed.

At the end, a room with a table and two chairs.

The suit was there.

He smiled like an old friend.

“Thank you for coming,” he said.

She sat without being told.

Her hands clenched in her lap to keep them from shaking.

The suit folded his hands.

“You’ve been under pressure,” he said gently. “It’s understandable.”

She said nothing.

The suit nodded, as if her silence was an admission.

“We’re not here to punish you,” he said. “We’re here to protect you.”

She stared at him.

“From what,” she whispered.

The suit’s smile thinned.

“From the kind of mistakes that ruin young lives,” he said.

He slid a folder across the table.

Inside: screenshots, logs, access timestamps.

Her name in multiple columns.

The suit tapped the page lightly.

“You accessed material outside your role,” he said. “You shared it.”

Her throat tightened.

“I didn’t—”

The suit held up a hand, calm.

“Don’t,” he said. “Lying is the part that makes this worse.”

She felt her chest compress.

“What happens now,” she asked.

The suit leaned back.

“Now,” he said, “you take a break.”

She blinked.

“A break?”

The suit nodded.

“Administrative leave,” he said. “Your accounts will be suspended. Your devices will be held for review.”

Her breath caught.

“And if I don’t agree,” she whispered.

The suit’s voice stayed gentle.

“Then we reclassify what you did,” he said. “And it becomes criminal.”

Silence.

She understood then: there was no path through this room that didn’t lead where they wanted.

The suit slid a pen toward her.

“Sign,” he said.

Her hand hovered.

Her conscience screamed.

Her fear answered louder.

She signed.

The suit took the paper, nodded with practiced satisfaction.

“Good,” he said. “Now. One more thing.”

She looked up.

The suit’s eyes sharpened.

“You will not contact anyone,” he said. “You will not post anything. You will not speak to press.”

He paused.

“And you will forget,” he said softly, “that you ever saw a ring finger tap.”

Her stomach turned.

She stood slowly on legs that didn’t feel like hers.

The suit smiled again.

“We’ll drive you home,” he said.

She blinked.

“I can walk.”

The suit’s smile didn’t change.

“No,” he said. “You can’t.”

That afternoon, her name vanished.

Her accounts logged out everywhere.
Her badge didn’t work.
Her email bounced.

Even her friends’ messages to her began to fail—sent, but never delivered.

It wasn’t a dramatic disappearance.

It was worse.

A soft deletion.

And in the room with the lamp and the Haitian radio humming low, One got a message from the librarian contact:

Raw file received. Source went dark immediately after. Likely compromised.

Sandi’s face went pale.

“We have to protect her,” she said.

One stared at the message.

He felt the familiar fire rise—the desire to react, to rage, to burn the feed down with truth.

Then he remembered the rule.

Don’t chase the feed.

Build the test.

He looked at Sandi.

“We can’t save her with noise,” he said.

Sandi’s eyes hardened.

“Then how.”

One’s voice was steady.

“We make her disappearance visible without naming her,” he said. “We force the regime to choose: admit they control edits… or admit they punish witnesses.”

Sandi exhaled sharply.

“Release the raw,” she said.

One nodded.

“But strategically,” he replied. “So it protects her, not exposes her.”
