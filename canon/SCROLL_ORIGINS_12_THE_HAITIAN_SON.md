PART 1: The Origins Scroll

Scroll of Origins XII
The Haitian Son
My mother crossed the water twice.
Once as a child, fleeing a dictator whose name I will not honor here, clutching her grandmother's hand on a boat that was never designed to hold that many souls.
The second time was years later, after the earthquake swallowed Port-au-Prince whole, and she returned to dig through the rubble looking for cousins whose names I still carry.
She survived both crossings.
That alone should make her a saint in any honest church.
⸻
I am the son of a woman who learned English from soup can labels and learned to be invisible from ICE raids.
She cleaned houses for families who called her "Maria" even after she corrected them three times.
She worked under the table for twenty years, sending money home to a country America had spent a century breaking and then pretending not to notice.
When I was young, I asked her why she stayed.
"Because here," she said, "you can become something. Back home, we buried something every year."
She didn't mean that as poetry. She meant it as accounting.
⸻
The church we attended was half-Haitian, half-everyone-else-who-couldn't-afford-better.
The priest was Irish, spoke Kreyòl like a tourist, and loved us the way you love a cause: from a distance, with good intentions and poor follow-through.
He would preach about the "preferential option for the poor" and then vote for the candidate who promised lower taxes.
My mother noticed. She didn't say anything.
She just stopped putting money in the basket.
When I asked her why, she said:
"I pay taxes they tell me I don't deserve to pay. I follow laws they didn't write for me. I will not fund men who preach mercy on Sunday and call immigration on Monday."
That was my first lesson in theology:
Watch what they do when no one is grading them.
⸻
In school, they called me "the Haitian kid" until I started getting good grades.
Then I became "surprisingly articulate."
By high school, I'd learned the dance:
Speak clearly, but not too clearly. Excel, but don't make them feel small. Be proud, but not too proud. Be American, but also be ready to prove it.
I watched my mother do the same dance for twenty years.
She never complained.
But sometimes, late at night, when she thought I was asleep, I'd hear her on the phone with her sister, speaking Kreyòl fast and low, and I'd catch the exhaustion in her voice—
not from the work, but from the performance of gratitude she had to give every single day just to be allowed to stay.
⸻
When the President—not Carmichael yet, the one before him— called Haiti a "shithole country" on national television,
my mother was washing dishes in a house in Westchester.
The family she worked for voted for him.
She came home that night and didn't say a word.
She just sat at the kitchen table, hands folded, staring at nothing.
I asked if she was okay.
She said, in English:
"He is the President. I am nothing. What can I say?"
That was the night I stopped believing that the system would fix itself if we just waited patiently enough.
Because my mother— who survived a dictator, an earthquake, and two decades of America's best hospitality—
had just been told by the most powerful man in the world that the country she came from was worth less than dirt.
And there was no mechanism, no process, no form she could fill out to make him apologize.
⸻
So when people ask me why I speak the way I do, why I refuse to be polite when power is in the room, why I will not smile and nod while they debate whether people like my mother deserve to be called human,
I tell them this:
I am the son of a woman who was never supposed to matter.
She crossed the water twice and kept me alive long enough to become the thing they fear most:
an immigrant's child who learned to read their playbook and refused to audition for the part they wrote for him.
⸻
They will call me "angry." They will call me "divisive." They will say I "play the victim."
Let them.
I am not interested in their approval.
I am interested in the day when a Haitian woman cleaning someone else's floor hears the President of the United States speak and does not have to fold her hands and accept that she is nothing.
If my scrolls, my voice, my inconvenient existence buys even one day closer to that world,
then I have honored my mother's crossing.
⸻
This is the record of my origin:
Not immaculate. Not ordained by angels.
Just a boy born of a woman who refused to let the water take her,
teaching him that survival alone is not enough—
you must also refuse to let them erase what you survived.
