Scene: The Counterfeit Aid

The first person to take the White House “Community Stability Program” wasn’t naïve.

She was tired.

A single mom named Keisha—two jobs, one kid, one rent check away from disaster—had been attending a witness circle quietly. She didn’t speak much. She listened. She took the checklist home and used it like an allergy test on her feed.

But after the barber got hit with inspections and the teacher got put under review, Keisha’s fear shifted:

Not fear of the regime.

Fear of being collateral.

Then a flyer appeared in her building lobby.

Official seal. Clean typography.

COMMUNITY STABILITY PROGRAM
Support for families impacted by online harassment.

It promised:
	•	legal guidance
	•	emergency assistance
	•	“privacy-protecting support”

Keisha laughed at that last line, bitter.

But her kid needed new shoes.
And her landlord was raising rent.

So she went.

The “support office” was in a rented storefront with soft lighting and smiling people wearing lanyards.

A woman greeted her warmly.

“Thank you for coming,” she said. “We’re here to help.”

Keisha nodded, guarded.

The woman handed her a form.

Name.
Address.
Employer.
School.
Phone.
Emergency contacts.
Social media handles.

Keisha paused.

“Why you need all that,” she asked.

The woman smiled, gentle, rehearsed.

“So we can protect you,” she said. “So we can respond quickly if something happens.”

Keisha hesitated.

“What if I don’t want to give it,” she asked.

The woman’s smile tightened just slightly.

“Then we can’t enroll you,” she said. “We have to verify eligibility.”

Keisha looked around.

Other people filling forms.

No one looked happy. Just desperate.

Keisha swallowed and signed.

She received a small grocery card and a pamphlet about “healthy media consumption.”

She went home half-relieved, half-sick.

Two days later, her supervisor called her into a glass office.

HR sat beside him.

Keisha’s stomach dropped.

Her supervisor cleared his throat.

“Keisha,” he said, “we’ve received concerns about your outside activities.”

Keisha blinked.

“What outside activities,” she asked.

HR slid a printed screenshot across the desk.

A photo from the witness circle—taken from the back of the room, grainy.

Keisha’s face wasn’t perfectly clear, but clear enough.

Beneath it, a caption:

EMPLOYEE ATTENDING EXTREMIST MISINFORMATION MEETINGS

Keisha’s hands went cold.

“That’s not extremist,” she said. “It’s media literacy. Verification.”

HR’s voice was polite.

“We’re not making a judgment,” she said. “We’re responding to risk.”

Keisha stared at her.

“You’re firing me,” she said.

Her supervisor looked away.

“We’re placing you on unpaid leave pending review,” he said, using the same words the district man used on Ms. Rivera.

Pending.

Ambiguity.

The soft blade.

Keisha left the building trembling.

Outside, her phone buzzed.

A text from an unknown number:

We tried to help you. You chose the wrong people.

Keisha’s knees almost gave out.

She sat on a curb and stared at the city like it had become a machine.

In the witness circle channel—offline routed, trusted tree—she sent a short message:

I took the Stability help. Now I’m on unpaid leave. They had a photo from the circle. I’m sorry.

A minute later, Helen called her.

Not a text. A call.

Keisha answered with a choked voice.

“I messed up,” she said. “I’m sorry. I’m so sorry.”

Helen’s voice was calm and firm.

“You’re not the problem,” Helen said. “The trap is the problem.”

Keisha sobbed once, quietly, ashamed.

Helen continued:

“Listen to me,” she said. “This is why we built the mutual aid branch. You’re not alone.”

Keisha swallowed.

“They have my data,” she whispered.

Helen’s voice hardened.

“Then we document,” she said. “We don’t panic. We document, and we support you.”

Keisha’s breath shook.

“What do I do,” she asked.

Helen’s answer came like a hand on the shoulder:

“You come to the next circle,” she said. “Not to be punished. To be held.”
