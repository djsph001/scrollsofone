Scene: Sandi Between Two Voices

The clip found her before the staff did.

It slid into her feed between a sponsored maternity brand and a grainy video of a basketball trick shot. No caption, no tag, just a thumbnail of One in a badly lit hall, eyes ringed with exhaustion and something harder.

She almost scrolled past.

Then she saw the quote stamped across the bottom in white:

I am the voice that refused to be edited.

Sandi tapped it.

The sound hit her all at once—One’s voice, unpolished, a little hoarse, that strange mix of anger and tenderness she’d felt first in his late-night messages before the rallies, before the pregnancy, before the world understood there was something between them that wasn’t just political.

She watched the whole thing without blinking.

“I will not help you turn this crisis into a season premiere.
I am not your rating spike.
I am not your villain-of-the-week…”

Her hand drifted to her stomach without thinking. Twelve weeks. The doctors had called it “early second trimester.” Her mother had called it “too soon to tell anyone.” Cable news had called it “unconfirmed rumors of a scandal.”

The baby kicked—not a real kick, not yet, more like a roll or a flutter—but enough to make her aware of her body in the chair. Real, present, here.

The door clicked.

“Ms. Carmichael?”

Her aide, Lila, leaned into the office like a question mark.

“You’re trending,” Lila said.

Sandi locked her phone and set it face-down.

“For what this time?” she asked.

Lila stepped in, tablet in hand. “Two things. One—” she grimaced at her own word choice “—the networks are pushing a split screen: your father at the podium inviting the debate, and One refusing. They’re framing it as ‘Power vs. Prophecy.’ You’re in the B-roll for both.”

“And the second thing?” Sandi asked.

Lila hesitated. “You… liked his speech. For twenty-three minutes. From your official account.”

Sandi’s chest tightened. “Already pulled back.”

“Yeah. But screenshots exist. They always do.”

Of course they did.

Lila slid the tablet across the desk. On the screen was a draft statement, already formatted in the familiar clean font of the West Wing press office.

“I respect the office of the President and believe that in times of division, leaders must choose dialogue over spectacle…”

It was like reading a ghost of herself that never existed.

“Whose language is this?” Sandi asked.

“Technically yours,” Lila said. “They sent it over as ‘suggested framing.’ Your comms team put your name in first person so you could ‘make it your own.’”

Of course they did.

Sandi read it to the end. It never mentioned One by name. It never mentioned the Baptist, or synthetic prophets, or anything real. Just “dialogue,” “unity,” “hope,” the disinfected vocabulary of a nation perpetually “at a crossroads.”

“They want this out within the hour,” Lila added gently. “Your father’s office is assuming you’ll cooperate.”

“‘Cooperate,’” Sandi repeated softly.

Lila shifted her weight. “Look, off the record—I don’t think you should sign this as-is. It’s too on-the-nose. People know you’re… they know you and One have history. It’ll read like hostage copy.”

Sandi stared at her.

“Do you believe him?” she asked.

“Who?”

“One.”

Lila swallowed. “About what?”

“The synthetic prophets. The Baptist. Any of it.”

Lila looked at the closed door, then back at Sandi. When she spoke, her voice was low.

“I believe the government runs programs I will never see briefing books for,” she said. “And I believe your father’s people are good at calling things ‘classification’ when they mean ‘political risk.’”

“That’s not an answer,” Sandi said.

Lila held her gaze. “I believe your instincts are better than their memos.”

For a moment, Sandi wanted to cry. Not because of One, or her father, or the baby, but because of how rare it felt to hear someone say that out loud in this building.

Instead she exhaled and let the feeling pass through.

“Okay,” she said. “Here’s what we’re going to do.”

Lila straightened.

“I’ll give them something they can live with,” Sandi said. “But it’s going to say what I mean. Not what they wish I meant.”

She picked up a pen, glanced at the tablet, and began crossing out lines—whole sentences, entire paragraphs, not tweaking but amputating.

The phrase ‘I respect the office of the President’ had a neat little line drawn through it. In the margin, she wrote:

Respect is not the same as obedience.

Lila watched, eyes widening slightly.

Sandi replaced “dialogue over spectacle” with:

“truth over theater, wherever it is spoken.”

She crossed out “I urge all parties to de-escalate” and wrote:

“I refuse to pretend that insisting on transparency is the same as sowing division.”

When she was done, the “statement” was something else entirely—shorter, sharper, nearly dangerous.

She handed the tablet back.

“Send this version to his office,” she said.

“They’re not going to like it,” Lila murmured.

“They’re not supposed to,” Sandi said. “They’re supposed to decide whether they want my name or their words. They can’t have both.”

Lila hesitated. “And if they push back?”

“Tell them I’m in a ‘reflection window’ and can’t be reached for revisions,” Sandi said.

“Is that a thing?” Lila asked.

“It is now.”

A small, unexpected laugh escaped Lila. “You know they’re going to call you,” she said.

“I won’t answer.”

“And if the President himself calls?”

Sandi looked at her phone on the desk. For a second she imagined his name lighting up the screen: Dad, not Mr. President, not Commander-in-Chief, just the man who used to fall asleep on the couch with campaign memos on his chest and call it “rest.”

“If he calls,” she said slowly, “I’ll answer. And I’ll tell him the truth.”

She picked up her phone and unlocked it again. The clip was still open where she’d left it, paused on One’s final line:

I will already be there, with them.

Her thumb hovered over the share button.

“For the record,” Lila said, “I’m not advising you to touch that.”

“I know,” Sandi said.

She didn’t re-like it. She did something worse, from the regime’s point of view: she copied the link, opened a private group chat with six old friends from college—not political operatives, not staff, just people who remembered her before she became “the First Daughter” or “the President’s adult child”—and pasted it there with a single line:

“Tell me honestly: does he sound crazy to you?”

Three dots appeared almost instantly as someone started typing.

Behind her, the city outside the window glowed with screens she couldn’t see—phones, projectors, anonymous walls carrying the phrase the voice that refused to be edited into places no comms director could fully map.

The baby rolled again. Sandi pressed her hand lightly against the movement, as if to say: I know. I feel it too.

“Anything else for now?” she asked.

Lila checked her notes. “There’s a request from state media for you to appear tomorrow and ‘clarify your support for the President’s moral leadership.’”

Sandi looked out at the city.

“Tell them I’m busy,” she said.

“With what?” Lila asked automatically.

Sandi smiled, tired but real.

“Growing the future,” she said. “They can wait.”
