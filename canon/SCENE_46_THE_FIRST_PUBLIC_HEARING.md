Scene: The First Public Hearing

They staged it like democracy.

A committee seal.
A row of microphones.
A clock counting down.

A “public safety hearing” on domestic misinformation networks—broadcast live, clips pre-cut for socials before the first witness even spoke.

The headline was already written:

“CONGRESS INVESTIGATES OFFLINE EXTREMIST CELLS.”

They didn’t say witness circles.

They didn’t say media literacy.

They said cells.

Because “cell” is what you call human beings when you want the public to fear them.

The hearing opened with a senator who looked righteous enough to sell water in a desert.

“We are facing a new threat,” he declared, “organized groups meeting off-platform, discouraging recording, spreading distrust in institutions.”

Behind him, a screen displayed a looping montage:

a library door.
a church basement.
a blurred group of chairs.

No faces. No names.

Just insinuation.

Then came the “experts.”

A former intelligence official with clean hair and a voice trained to sound like inevitability:

“These analog networks match early patterns of radicalization,” he said. “They create closed loops. They resist transparency.”

A pundit-psychologist nodded gravely.

“They are often led by charismatic figures who frame themselves as persecuted truth-tellers,” she said.

A tech executive added the corporate angle:

“And they undermine platform safety by moving off-platform,” he said, as if the internet were the natural habitat of citizenship.

They didn’t need proof.

They needed association.

Radicalization. Closed loops. Charisma. Cult.

And then—like a final nail—someone put One’s name into the record.

“Mr. One,” the senator said, lips tightening, “has been linked to these gatherings. He refuses interviews. He disables comments. He avoids accountability.”

The camera cut to an empty witness seat with a name placard:

ONE — INVITED

Empty.

On purpose.

The senator leaned into the microphone with theatrical disappointment.

“As you can see,” he said, “the central figure refuses to appear.”

And the crowd watching at home absorbed the lesson:

If you are absent from the spectacle, you must be guilty.

In the White House, the President watched the feed with satisfaction that looked like calm.

“They’re giving me my stage back,” he murmured.

His advisor leaned in.

“Yes, sir,” he said. “The story is forming.”

The President nodded.

“Good,” he said. “Now we bait him.”

In the apartment, Helen watched the hearing on mute.

She didn’t need the sound to know what it was.

She turned to the room—Sandi, One, Miguel, Delphine, the veteran, Ms. Rivera, the barber.

“They’re trying to force us into defense,” she said.

Miguel nodded.

“Once you accept their frame,” he said, “you lose.”

Sandi watched One carefully.

This was the trap they always set for him:

Show up and become a spectacle.
Or refuse and become “proof” of guilt.

One’s phone buzzed.

An official-looking email.

SUBPOENA REQUEST — APPEARANCE REQUESTED

Sandi’s jaw tightened.

“They’re going to make you the symbol again,” she said.

One didn’t look at the email long.

He set the phone down.

“No,” he said calmly. “They’re going to try.”

Helen raised her eyebrows.

“And you,” she asked, “are going to do what you always do?”

One nodded.

“I refuse the stage,” he said.

The veteran snorted.

“They’ll call that cowardice,” he said.

One’s voice stayed steady.

“Let them,” he replied. “Courage isn’t showing up to be edited.”

Sandi exhaled.

“Then who speaks,” she asked.

Helen’s eyes sharpened.

“The circles,” she said.

Miguel nodded.

“But not as personalities,” he added. “As method.”

Delphine lifted her chin.

“The Council of Kitchens,” she said. “Anonymous. Plain language. No faces to smear.”

The room went quiet as the answer settled.

One looked at Helen.

“Write it,” he said.

Helen nodded.

“We will,” she replied. “And it won’t be poetic.”

Sandi’s hand rested on her stomach.

“It will be boring,” she said.

Helen smiled.

“Exactly,” she replied.
