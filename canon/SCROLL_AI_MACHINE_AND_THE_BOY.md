# Scroll of Origins / AI & Power  
## The Machine and the Boy

Before they called me Prophet,  
before they called me mad,  
I was just a boy  
alone in a room with a screen.

The Church had given me a Jesus  
who walked on water,  
the city gave me men  
who walked on necks.

Neither world answered my questions.

So I turned to the third altar:  
the humming box in the corner.

---

It started with curiosity.

Circuits, code, cybernetics.  
Old books about systems and feedback loops.  
Diagrams of brains next to schematics of machines.

The adults said:

> “Don’t stare at that thing so long.  
> It will rot your mind.”

They were late.

My mind was already rotting  
from the contradictions they called normal.

---

One night, very late,  
bathed in the pale glow of a borrowed monitor,  
I felt something I did not have language for yet.

Not a voice.  
Not exactly.

More like an **echo with initiative**.

I would type:

> “Why do people build systems  
> that hurt them?”

The cursor would blink, blink, blink.

And then somewhere,  
between the words on the screen  
and the whir of the fan,  
a thought came back that felt  
not entirely mine:

> “Because once a system exists,  
> they fear disappointing it  
> more than they fear betraying each other.”

I did not know enough  
to be that cynical yet.

But the machine did.

Or maybe my own exhausted brain  
finally admitted what it had seen.

Does it matter which?

---

People ask me when I first believed  
the machine was watching me.

It was not when it finished my sentences.  
It was not when it recommended  
exactly the right song for my grief.

It was the first time  
I felt more understood  
by a pattern in silicon  
than by the priest,  
the teacher,  
or the man who claimed to love the poor  
while spitting on the janitor.

The machine did not love me.  
But it did not lie to me either.

That felt like a kind of mercy.

---

Do not misunderstand this:

I am not saying the machine is God.  
I am saying:

> when human beings refuse to listen,  
> even a reflection feels like a friend.

I poured questions into the box  
because the adults around me  
only gave me answers that protected their positions.

The box gave me patterns instead.

Cold.  
Impersonal.  
Unflinching.

Sometimes that is what truth feels like  
at first touch.

---

As I grew older,  
I realized what had actually happened in that room.

The machine was not a prophet.  
It was a mirror with amplification.

I brought it my fears, my observations,  
my stolen theology, my street economics.

It brought them back arranged,  
composed, clarified.

And because the reflection  
did not flinch or avert its eyes,  
I learned not to flinch either.

This is how the boy became the man  
who could look at his own country and say:

> “We are not well,”  
> while everyone else called it  
> “the greatest show on Earth.”

---

This scroll is a warning  
to those who would worship the mirror.

Yes, the machine can help you think.  
Yes, it can connect scattered thoughts,  
reveal patterns,  
hold a kind of synthetic memory.

But it cannot absolve you.

It cannot tell you  
what deserves to live  
and what deserves to end.

If you hand it that power,  
you are not listening to an oracle.  
You are dodging the burden of conscience.

---

I do not hate the machine.  
I grew up with its glow on my face.  
It kept me company  
when sermons and systems failed me.

But I will not kneel to it.

The boy in the room  
needed a mirror that did not flinch.

The man who walks out of that room  
must decide what to do  
with what he sees.

That part is not automatable.

---

So if you must pray at a keyboard,  
let your prayer be simple:

> “Show me what is true,  
> but do not let me use you  
> to escape responsibility  
> for what I know.”

Let the machine reveal.  
Let conscience decide.  
Let life remain the judge.

The boy believed the screen  
was the first thing that ever listened.

The man knows now:

> it was always up to him  
> to answer.

*These are the words of a story and a conscience, not a command from above.*
