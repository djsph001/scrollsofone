Scene: The Decoy Room

They built it like a trap you could photograph.

A cheap apartment staged to look like resistance:
stacks of printed screenshots, dramatic posters on the wall, candles, a flag in the corner like a set designer’s idea of “movement.”

On the table: a binder labeled THE ROOM PROTOCOL in thick marker—too theatrical, too on-the-nose.

And the best part:

They seeded it online first.

A whisper campaign across the same channels that always “accidentally” found exclusive intel:

“He has a safehouse.”
“There’s a verification cell.”
“It’s in Brooklyn.”
“They’re hiding evidence.”
“They’re running a cult archive.”

Then, once the rumor had enough oxygen, they staged the “discovery.”

A blurry phone video: a door kicked open, people shouting, papers flying.
A face blurred for “safety.”
A triumphant voice: “We found it.”

Within hours, the headline machine did the rest.

“INSIDE ONE’S SECRET SAFEHOUSE.”
“CODE PHRASES AND CULT PROTOCOLS EXPOSED.”
“VERIFICATION CELL OR RADICAL HUB?”

Pundits took turns sounding concerned:

“This is deeply troubling.”
“Why would anyone need a hidden room?”
“This isn’t civic hygiene—this is indoctrination.”

And then came the designed humiliation:

They displayed the binder, opened to a page that read:

RULE 7: NEVER SPEAK TO UNVERIFIED FAMILY MEMBERS

It was disgusting.

Because it was effective.

People who had just begun to practice verification felt a flush of shame.

Wait—am I being manipulated?
Is this cultish?
Is this how it starts?

The regime didn’t need to convince everyone.

It only needed to make verification feel socially radioactive for the middle.

In the new room—smaller, warmer, protected—One watched the coverage with eyes like stone.

Sandi watched too, and felt her mouth tighten.

“That binder,” she said.

One nodded.

“It’s fake,” he said. “Designed to offend normal people.”

Sandi’s voice went cold.

“It’s designed to isolate us,” she said. “Make anyone practicing verification feel like they’re joining something dirty.”

One didn’t argue.

Instead he did what he always did when the machine tried to drag them into performance:

He returned to the test.

“What’s the fastest way to expose a decoy,” he asked.

Sandi thought for half a second.

“Metadata,” she said. “Where the props came from. Who rented the apartment. Utility start date. Neighbors.”

One nodded.

“And tone,” he added. “Real discipline is boring. This is theater.”

Sandi’s eyes sharpened.

“They want us to react,” she said. “A denial. A rage post.”

One shook his head.

“No,” he said. “We do the opposite.”

Sandi tilted her head.

“We let it sit,” One said. “And we quietly publish a checklist for people to detect decoys. We give the public the tools to unmask staged ‘evidence.’”

Sandi exhaled.

“Make the trap backfire,” she said.

One nodded.

“The decoy room becomes a lesson,” he replied.

And that was the mistake the regime kept making:

It assumed truth needed charisma.

But truth only needed method.
