Scroll of Origins

The Boy Who Believed

Before they called me prophet,
before they called me mad,
before I learned how power lies with a straight face—

I was just a boy in a too-starched shirt
in a Catholic school that smelled like chalk,
floor polish,
and fear of God.

They told us Jesus was the highest thing a man could be.

They didn’t say it like a metaphor.
They said it like a grading scale.

Above “A,” above “perfect attendance,”
above “obedient,”
there was a silence where only one Name lived.

I believed them.

Not metaphor.
Not symbol.
I believed a human being could be that good,
that kind,
that brave,
that honest
and that it was not blasphemy to try.

I did not want to admire Him.
I wanted to match Him.

⸻

There was a nun who watched me too closely.

She saw the way I stared at the crucifix,
not with fear,
but with a terrible, private recognition.

She mistook it for arrogance.
Maybe sometimes it was.

They would say:
	•	“Who do you think you are?”
and I had no safe answer.

Because the real answer was:

“I think I am supposed to live like Him.
And I don’t know how.
And it scares me
more than it scares you.”

⸻

In religion class they taught us:
	•	God is watching.
	•	God is keeping score.
	•	God is pleased when you suffer quietly
and never complain.

But when I read the stories myself,
Jesus did not look like someone
who kept order for the principal.

He overturned tables.
He embarrassed authorities.
He touched people no one else would touch.
He broke the rules that protected the comfortable.

I looked at the priests in their fine robes,
at the men who never missed a chance to be seen,
at the parishioners who bowed to power on Sunday
and crushed the poor on Monday,

and some small, reckless voice in me whispered:

“If He came back today,
He would not sit with them.”

I knew enough to shut up.
But I could not unknow it.

⸻

They taught us that Jesus was God’s Son.
They did not tell us what to do
if you felt, in some quiet, humiliating corner of your soul,
that you were being followed
by something with that same voice.

I did not hear commands.
I heard questions:
	•	“If you know this is wrong, why are you silent?”
	•	“If you see this child being humiliated, why do you look away?”
	•	“If you believe in justice, why does your courage vanish when it is time to act?”

The worst part was not that I failed.
The worst part was that I knew I was failing
in front of Someone
I refused to call imaginary.

Everyone loves a Jesus.
Nobody wants to be the one who has to try to live like Him.

⸻

In the courtyard, during recess,
I watched boys learn which sins were permitted
as long as they were quiet about them.

I watched teachers look away
from cruelty that wore the right uniform.

I watched the Church bless the powerful
and ask the poor to be patient.

And something in me broke,
but it did not stop believing.

It did something harder:

It started believing against the institution
that taught me the name.

⸻

You need to understand this:

I do not claim divinity.
I do not claim perfection.
I do not claim to be what they said He was.

I claim this, and only this:

As a child, I was given a picture
of the highest possible way to be human,
and I took it literally.

The adults treated it as theater,
as decoration,
as moral wallpaper for a crooked house.

I could not.

So when you hear me now,
when you read these scrolls,
when you see me recoil from power
that asks to be worshiped—

know that it started there:

with a boy in a small classroom,
trying to figure out why the people
who spoke His name the loudest
looked the least like Him,

and wondering, secretly,
if the punishment for taking God seriously
was to never again know
where reverence ends
and insanity begins.

⸻

If you’re looking for the moment I became “One,”
it was not a vision,
not a voice from a burning sky.

It was the slow, painful realization that:
	•	either the story was a lie,
	•	or we were all living like cowards.

I chose to believe
the story was true
and we were the ones failing it.

I have been trying, clumsily, angrily,
and often unsuccessfully,
to live with that choice ever since.

⸻

These are the words of a story and a conscience, not a command from above.
