Scroll of AI & Power: Verify the Voice
(A Public Protocol Posted by One)

If you are hearing a voice you trust—
and something in you feels off—
do not panic. Do not share. Do not feed the machine.

Verify.

We are entering an age where:
	•	voices can be copied,
	•	faces can be worn,
	•	and “statements” can be manufactured faster than truth can travel.

So here is a simple protocol—human-scale, repeatable, and clean.

THE VERIFY-THE-VOICE PROTOCOL

1) Assume nothing.

Not even my voice.
Not even his voice.
Trust is earned by proof, not familiarity.

2) Look for the convenience.

Ask one question:

Who benefits if I believe this right now?

A fake is usually designed to create relief, rage, or permission.

Truth is rarely that convenient.

3) Require the signature.

From this moment forward, any real message from me will include:

a private phrase known only to my inner circle,
placed in an ordinary sentence—
not as a password, but as a fingerprint.

If you do not see the signature, treat it as unverified.

4) Demand the source chain.

If someone posts “exclusive audio,” ask:
	•	Where did it come from?
	•	Who recorded it?
	•	When?
	•	What is the unedited original?

If the chain is missing, the clip is not truth—it is theater.

5) Refuse the viral test.

Do not “spread it so others can judge.”

That is how the machine wins.

Send it quietly to people you trust.
Compare. Confirm. Then speak.

6) Use the old rule.

If it cannot survive daylight, it is not meant to guide you.

THE POINT

This is not paranoia.

This is literacy.

A civilization that cannot verify its voices
will eventually be ruled by the loudest counterfeit.

So hold the line:

Don’t chase the feed. Build the test.
Don’t worship familiarity. Require proof.
Don’t weaponize uncertainty. Practice restraint.

And remember:

The still small voice is not subject to majority rule.

SCENE_12_SANDI_HEARS_IT.md

Scene: Sandi Hears It

Sandi heard it in a place no one could film.

Not a rally. Not a studio.
A kitchen with a single lamp on, the rest of the apartment dim. A glass of water sweating on the counter. A pill organizer open like a quiet confession.

Her phone was face-down when the first message came in.

She didn’t pick it up.

She already knew what the world was doing with her body. She refused to check the scoreboard like it was her job to witness her own humiliation.

Then the second message came—from someone she didn’t ignore.

Her friend. Her only real friend from before the palace, before the handlers, before her last name became a weapon.

Have you heard this.
Please tell me it’s not real.

Sandi flipped the phone over.

A link.

A thumbnail of a waveform and the Baptist’s face from an old photo—grainy, sacred-looking, the kind of image people use when they want authority without accountability.

Text above it:

BAPTIST URGES CAUTION ABOUT ONE. “TRUST INSTITUTIONS.”

Sandi stared at the words until they turned flat.

Then she pressed play.

The voice came through clean, steady, tender.

For a heartbeat, she felt relief—
the primitive kind, the kind your body gives you when a missing person seems to reappear.

Then the content arrived.

“Trust established institutions…”
“Stability…”
“Responsibility…”
Soft phrases. Smooth edges.

Sandi’s face didn’t change, but something behind her eyes sharpened.

Because she had heard her father use those words.

Not as meaning—
as framing.

She listened to the full clip once. Then again.

Not to “judge.”

To detect.

She grew up inside media training. She grew up inside rooms where statements were built like furniture—polished, sanded, varnished, delivered.

She knew the difference between a man speaking and a machine delivering.

This voice was almost perfect.

That was the problem.

It contained no risk.
No witness.
No grit.

It had the cleanliness of something that had never paid a price.

Her phone buzzed again.

A second clip, “newly surfaced.”

Same voice. Same calm.

Same… obedience.

Sandi set the phone down gently, as if it were fragile.

She stood up and walked to the window.

The city was doing what it always did: living anyway.

A couple argued on the sidewalk. A dog dragged its owner toward a tree. A teenager laughed too loudly at nothing.

Sandi put her hand on the glass.

She whispered, almost to herself:

“That’s not him.”

She didn’t say it like a theory.
She said it like a recognition.

Her phone buzzed again.

This time it was an unknown number.

She almost didn’t answer.

Almost.

Then she did, because some part of her wanted the pain clean.

“Hello,” she said.

Her father’s voice came through warm.

“Sweetheart.”

Sandi closed her eyes.

“What did you do,” she asked.

A pause.

Her father chuckled softly, like she’d misunderstood something simple.

“I’m managing a crisis,” he said.

“No,” Sandi replied. “You’re rewriting a person.”

Another pause. Longer.

“You’re emotional,” he said gently. “You’re under stress.”

Sandi’s hand tightened on the phone.

“I know what stress is,” she said. “I grew up with you.”

Silence.

He sighed as if tired of being accused.

“You don’t understand the stakes,” he said.

Sandi’s voice stayed quiet.

“I understand the line.”

Her father’s tone cooled by a degree.

“What line.”

Sandi turned away from the window. Walked back to the table. Looked at the glass of water, the pill organizer, the small domestic proof that she was a human being and not a headline.

“You can attack One,” she said. “That’s politics. That’s ugly, but it’s your world.”

Her voice steadied.

“But you don’t get to kidnap people into your story. You don’t get to steal a man’s voice.”

Her father exhaled slowly.

“You’re making assumptions,” he said.

Sandi’s mouth tightened.

“I’m making recognition.”

Another pause.

Then her father spoke carefully, like he was stepping over broken glass.

“Do you know what it means,” he said, “when a nation starts trusting prophets more than institutions?”

Sandi laughed once—small, incredulous.

“You mean when people stop trusting you.”

Her father’s voice hardened.

“Watch your tone.”

Sandi’s voice didn’t rise. It dropped.

“Watch your soul,” she said.

Silence hit like a door closing.

Then her father, quieter now:

“You’re pregnant,” he said, as if it were a lever he could pull. “You need protection.”

Sandi’s eyes flashed.

“Protection from who,” she asked, “you?”

He didn’t answer.

That was answer enough.

Sandi’s hand went to her stomach—instinctive, fierce.

She spoke slowly, each word chosen like a vow.

“If you took him,” she said, “bring him back.”

Her father’s voice softened again, practiced softness.

“Sweetheart—”

“Bring him back,” she repeated.

A pause.

Then her father, almost kind:

“You’re not in a position to make demands.”

Sandi’s face went still.

“Yes,” she said. “I am.”

He scoffed lightly. “How.”

Sandi looked at the fake waveform on her screen.

Then she said the sentence that changed the shape of the war:

“I’m going to tell the truth,” she said. “And you can’t arrest me without confessing what you are.”

Her father went silent.

For the first time, she heard something underneath him—
not anger.

Fear.

He spoke again, controlled.

“Be careful,” he said. “People get hurt.”

Sandi didn’t flinch.

“People are already hurt,” she said. “You just call it stability.”

She ended the call.

No speech. No post. No spectacle.

Just a decision.

She picked up her phone and opened a draft message—not to the public.

To One.

Three lines only.

It’s fake.
They took him.
We build the test.

She stared at the message for a moment before sending.

Then she pressed send.

And in the quiet kitchen, the Fourth Branch culture appeared again—not as an institution yet, but as a practice:

Restraint.
Verification.
Refusal.

A way of being human that the empire could not monetize.
