Scene: The Choice

The chair was comfortable on purpose.

Comfort is a tactic.

The lights were warm. The water was cold. The room smelled faintly of cedar, like someone had paid to make captivity feel civilized.

Across from the Baptist sat the man in the suit again, hands folded, expression gentle.

“You’ve been through a lot,” the man said.

The Baptist stared at him without blinking.

“You want sympathy,” the Baptist said. “Buy a dog.”

The man smiled as if the insult had been anticipated.

“We don’t want to harm you,” he said again. “We want to resolve this.”

“By stealing my voice,” the Baptist replied.

The man tilted his head slightly.

“By stabilizing the nation,” he corrected.

The Baptist leaned back.

“Let’s stop pretending,” he said. “You’re not stabilizing anything. You’re manufacturing permission.”

A pause.

The man in the suit slid a tablet across the table.

On it: One’s protocol post. The line about verification. The signature phrase, nested in plain text.

The man tapped the screen.

“He’s teaching people to distrust audio,” he said. “That’s dangerous.”

The Baptist’s eyes narrowed.

“Truth is dangerous to liars,” he said.

The man nodded as if acknowledging a weather report.

“Here is the offer,” he said.

He spoke the words like a banker.

“You will record one short video. Sixty seconds. You will look into the camera and tell the public you are safe. That you are not detained. That these impersonation rumors are lies.”

The Baptist laughed once, quiet.

“You want me to certify my own kidnapping,” he said.

“We want you to calm a public panic,” the man replied.

The Baptist looked at the tablet again, then up at the man.

“And if I refuse.”

The man’s face remained mild.

“Then we continue without you,” he said. “But the imitation will grow less… respectful.”

The Baptist’s jaw tightened.

“That’s a threat.”

“It’s a forecast,” the man said.

The Baptist sat in silence for a long moment.

He remembered One’s first counsel scene—how he’d tried to teach the boy not to become a product.

He remembered the words he’d said outside the bodega:

You can take my body, but you won’t get my witness.

Now they were offering him a trick:

One real clip…
to make a thousand fakes believable.

One authentic minute…
to crown the synthetic gospel.

The man in the suit leaned in, softer now.

“You’re a moral man,” he said. “You don’t want violence. You don’t want unrest. This is your chance to prevent chaos.”

The Baptist stared at him.

Then he said something simple:

“You don’t get to use peace as blackmail.”

The man’s eyes hardened slightly.

“You’re choosing conflict.”

The Baptist shook his head.

“No,” he said. “I’m choosing truth.”

The man sat back.

“You understand,” he said, “that you can’t win.”

The Baptist’s voice lowered.

“I’m not trying to win,” he said. “I’m trying to remain a human being.”

The man’s smile disappeared.

He tapped something on the tablet.

A new video appeared: a deepfake of the Baptist, speaking warmly, praising the President’s “wisdom,” condemning “radicals,” calling One “sincere but unstable.”

The Baptist’s stomach turned.

The man watched him watch it.

“This will go out tonight,” the man said. “Unless you give us the real thing.”

The Baptist’s hands tightened on the edge of the chair.

He closed his eyes for a second—not prayer, not surrender—calibration.

Then he opened them.

“Bring me the camera,” the Baptist said.

The man in the suit’s eyebrows lifted.

“Good,” he said. “Wise.”

The Baptist’s eyes stayed flat.

“I didn’t say yes,” he said.

The man paused.

The Baptist leaned forward, voice quiet, precise.

“I’ll record,” he said. “But not what you want.”

The man’s smile returned, cautious now.

“We control the edit.”

The Baptist nodded.

“I know,” he said. “That’s why I’m going to put something in it you can’t edit out without revealing you edited it.”

The man’s smile froze.

“What.”

The Baptist’s gaze sharpened like a blade.

“A test,” he said.

A pause.

Then he added, almost gently:

“And if you’re smart, you’ll let it run.”

The man in the suit stared at him, trying to decide whether he was bluffing.

The Baptist sat still.

And in that stillness, the real war reappeared:

Not a war of force.

A war of integrity under compression.
