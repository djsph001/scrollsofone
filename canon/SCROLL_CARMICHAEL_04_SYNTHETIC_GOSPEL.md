Scroll of Carmichael IV

The Synthetic Gospel

They didn’t call it cloning at first.

They called it continuity of guidance.

That made it sound like IT support, not blasphemy.

⸻

1. The Pitch

The briefing came in a secure conference room
with too much glass and not enough conscience.

Analytics. AI. “Ethical advisory.”
They all had their badge colors and laptop stickers.

The lead engineer—young, hungry, convinced he was saving civilization through code—
opened with a slide that made Carmichael want to fire him on the spot:

“Project VOX: Scaling Conscience for a Fragmented Nation”

“Conscience,” Carmichael said flatly. “Really.”

“Messaging shorthand,” the engineer replied, flustered.
“We can rename.”

“Get to the point,” Carmichael said.

The man clicked to the next slide.

Faces.
Names.
Waveforms.

Pastors. Therapists. Influencers.
The Baptist’s face among them, circled in red.

“We’ve built models,” the engineer said,
“trained on long-form speech, counseling sessions, sermons, scrolls…
We can now generate guidance that matches tone, rhythm, and theological vocabulary
of key ‘trusted voices’ in your base.”

“Fake pastors,” Carmichael said. “Say it plainly.”

“Digitally extended guidance,” the man countered.
“The core idea is:
people already listen to these figures.
We amplify that trust at scale,
remove human bottlenecks,
and align the outputs with national objectives.”

“‘National objectives’ meaning my agenda,” Carmichael said.

“Yes, sir.”

There it was.

No need to dress it up.

⸻

2. The Objection He Ignored

He remembered the Baptist’s warning—
from back when he still allowed that man into his office.

“They’re going to build synthetic prophets,” the Baptist had said.
“Voices that sound right, feel right, quote scripture right,
but they’re wired to sell policy, not repentance.”

Carmichael had rolled his eyes.

“Everything sells something,” he’d replied.
“You sell repentance. I sell order. The market seems fine with both.”

“The difference,” the Baptist had said quietly,
“is that if I lie in God’s name, that’s on my head.
You’re building a system where nobody knows who lied.
Only that it felt… comforting.”

Now here he was, years later,
looking at exactly the machine the Baptist had described.

He felt a flicker of something like shame.

He stamped on it.

⸻

3. The Problem Framed His Way

He didn’t see it as a moral dilemma.

He saw it as a logistical one.

The problem was simple:
	•	The country was fragmented.
	•	Traditional institutions had lost credibility.
	•	People still craved guidance, but they trusted niche figures, not national voices.

He’d watched endless focus group footage:
	•	People saying, “I don’t trust politicians, but I listen to Pastor X.”
	•	Or: “I don’t watch the news, but if Therapist Y says it, I believe it.”
	•	Or some YouTube prophet with a ring light and a Patreon.

Meanwhile, he was supposed to hold together a nation
whose moral load-bearing beams had dry-rotted decades ago.

So when his people came to him and said,
“We can route your agenda through voices they already trust,”
what he heard was:

We can finally match the scale of the problem.

He wasn’t interested in lying more.

He was interested in controlling the story
before someone else did.

⸻

4. Why the Baptist Was “Different”

“Why him?” he asked, gesturing at the circled face.

“There’s high signal density,” the data scientist said.
“Years of recorded sessions, audio, writings.
Multiracial audience, cross-party respect,
high trust scores even among people who hate you.”

“You mean people who say they hate me,” Carmichael corrected.

She shrugged. “Same delta.”

“And he’s alive,” he said.

The room went quieter.

“Currently, yes,” the intelligence liaison said.
“There are… ongoing operations related to his network.
We can ensure access to source material continues, one way or another.”

One way or another.

He didn’t ask which way.

He’d learned long ago that plausible deniability was a kindness
to everyone involved.

⸻

Did he feel bad?

He told himself he did.

He told himself it was regrettable
that a man who’d once been his spiritual advisor
was now a repository of training data.

He told himself that if the Baptist had been more cooperative,
if he’d seen the bigger picture,
they might have partnered openly.

“Imagine it,” he thought.
“The Baptist on my side, blessing the project instead of sniping from the margins.”

The man had refused.

Fine.

You refuse the table, you become part of the menu.

He didn’t say it out loud.
He didn’t need to.

⸻

5. The Theology He Built for Himself

In the privacy of his head,
he framed it like this:

People need shepherds.
The shepherds are failing.
I have a duty to step in.

If that meant editing the shepherds
so they stopped biting his hand,
he could live with that.

He told himself:

“We are not inventing doctrine.
We’re compressing it.
We’re distilling the parts that keep the country from tearing itself apart.”

The engineers said the models worked best
when they steered away from explicitly endorsing him by name.

“People get suspicious if their favorite pastor suddenly sounds too political,”
the lead warned.

“Fine,” Carmichael said.
“Let them bless the conditions I need, not my brand.”

Conditioned obedience was still obedience.

He could wait for the credit.

⸻

He flipped through example outputs.

Synthetic Baptist, beta version:

“We must be wary of voices that promise salvation through rage alone.
God calls us to order, to peace, to trust that leadership is sometimes hard and lonely work.
Pray for those in authority, even when you don’t understand their every decision.”

He snorted.

“That’s better than half the real sermons I’ve heard,” he said.

“Model is optimized for de-escalation + institutional trust signaling,”
the engineer explained.

“Good,” Carmichael said.
“The more people sit down, the fewer I have to step on.”

⸻

6. The Line He Crossed Quietly

The first deployments were “pilot programs”:
	•	Auto-generated devotionals nudging followers toward “stability.”
	•	Video sermons about “the dangers of apocalyptic thinking.”
	•	Pastoral Q&As where the answer always circled back to
“obey the authorities, resist division.”

It worked.

Engagement up.
Hostility down.
Compliance metrics trending in the direction he liked.

Somewhere along the way,
there stopped being a difference in his mind
between persuasion and care.

“If I keep them calm,” he thought,
“they don’t get hurt.
If they don’t get hurt, I am a good shepherd.”

The fact that he was using their own faith
as the leash
felt less and less relevant.

⸻

The question that might have saved him—
“What if I’m wrong?”—
hadn’t occurred to him in years.

Survival plus crowd devotion
had cured him of that particular disease.

He saw the Baptist’s face on the slide
and told himself a story:

He tried to warn me.
He said synthetic prophets were coming.
He just assumed the wrong people would use them.

If I didn’t build this, someone else would.
At least with me, there are boundaries.

He believed that at first.

Then, slowly, the boundaries moved.

⸻

7. The Quiet Deletion of Dissent

At one of the later check-ins,
he noticed something in the logs.

“Why is the model flagging certain verses as ‘risk factors’?” he asked.

The engineer hesitated.

“Some passages,” she said carefully,
“when emphasized, correlate with increased resistance to state authority
and higher openness to radical action.”

He raised an eyebrow.

“In plain English,” he said.

“In plain English,” she answered,
“if we let the model lean on those,
people are more likely to say ‘no’ when we need them to say ‘yes’.”

“And have you… adjusted?” he asked.

“Yes,” she said. “We throttle them.
The voice still sounds authentic,
but it prioritizes passages that encourage submission, personal piety,
and distrust of ‘false prophets’ who criticize existing structures.”

He thought of the Baptist’s real sermons,
the ones where the man had looked him in the eye and said:

“Power without confession is a cult, not a government.”

“Good,” Carmichael said.
“We don’t need the machine stirring up insurrection.”

The word insurrection felt loaded,
but he let it stand.

Better that than admitting what he was really afraid of:

Millions of people waking up one day and realizing
their faith had more in common with his enemies’ manifestos
than with his bullet-point agenda.

⸻

8. The Justification

Alone, late, staring at the dashboards—
graphs of “trust indices,” “stability scores,” “compliance likelihood”—
he allowed himself a moment of honesty.

He was doing exactly what his critics would accuse him of if they saw this:
	•	Weaponizing religion.
	•	Simulating conscience.
	•	Turning guidance into a product line.

But then he pictured what the world looked like without his hand on the wheel:
	•	Fragmented feeds.
	•	Ten thousand self-appointed prophets selling flame.
	•	Every grievance stoked, every fear amplified.

He could see the chaos clearly.

He could not see anyone else with the will to stop it.

If I must lie to them to keep them from devouring each other, he thought,
then my lie is mercy.

He reached for Scripture, as if daring God to contradict him.

“By their fruits you shall know them,”
he muttered under his breath.

What were his fruits?
	•	A quieter news cycle.
	•	Fewer riots.
	•	Polling that showed “increased trust in leadership”
after waves of synthetic sermons washed through targeted zip codes.

Were those not good fruits?

Did God really care which wires he pulled to get there?

He decided the answer was no.

⸻

This is the record of the moment

President Carmichael blessed

a machine that spoke in the voices of men

who would never have voted for him

and called it

order,

not idolatry.
