Scene: Carmichael Feels It

The President felt it before the data proved it.

He felt it the way a performer feels a crowd turning—not hostile, not loud—

cooler.

The feeds were still active. The outrage still came on schedule.
But something was missing:

the easy obedience.

He watched a clip of a pundit attacking “verification cults” and saw the comments beneath it shift.

Not everyone. Not even most.

But enough.

People asking for sources.
People posting hashes.
People linking the librarian report.

Even worse:

people refusing to fight.

Not defending One. Not praising Sandi.

Just… refusing to be moved.

A staffer entered with numbers.

“Sir,” she said carefully, “we’re seeing offline meetups.”

The President didn’t look away from the screen.

“Meetups,” he repeated.

“Yes,” she said. “Libraries. Churches. Teachers. Community centers. It’s spreading.”

The President’s jaw tightened.

“How,” he asked, voice flat.

The staffer hesitated. “They’re calling them—witness circles.”

The President finally turned.

His eyes were cold.

“Witness,” he said.

A pause.

“That’s a religious word,” he said.

The Chief of Staff stepped in, trying to keep it pragmatic.

“It’s basically media literacy groups,” he said. “Annoying, but not illegal.”

The President stared at him like he’d spoken in a child’s language.

“You don’t understand,” the President said softly.

He stood and walked to the window.

“Media literacy is a hobby,” he said. “Witness is a culture.”

He turned back.

“And cultures outlast administrations.”

Silence.

The digital director spoke, nervous.

“We’re flooding them,” she said. “We’re pushing more content, more raw clips, more—”

The President raised a hand.

“Floods don’t work on people who leave the river,” he said.

He paced slowly.

“They’re moving offline because they’ve learned the feed is compromised,” he said. “They’re building verification habits in rooms we can’t reach.”

The Chief of Staff frowned.

“So what do we do,” he asked.

The President stopped pacing.

He looked at them, voice calm enough to terrify.

“We make offline feel unsafe,” he said.

The Counsel stiffened.

“Sir—”

The President’s eyes narrowed.

“No raids,” he said. “No headlines. Just… accidents.”

A long silence.

The staffer swallowed.

“Accidents,” she echoed.

The President nodded once.

“Fire codes,” he said. “Permit problems. Noise complaints. Anonymous threats that force cancellations.”

He leaned forward slightly.

“And if that doesn’t work,” he said, “we give them a reason to fear being in a room with strangers.”

The Chief of Staff’s face tightened.

“You mean—”

The President cut him off with a look.

“I mean we remind them what chaos looks like,” he said. “And then we offer stability again.”

He sat down.

The room exhaled without meaning to.

The President looked at the screen again—comments asking for sources like tiny stones in his shoe.

Then he said the sentence that revealed the true wound:

“They’re taking my stage away,” he said quietly.

No one replied.

Because they all understood:

This wasn’t about One.

It was about control of the audience.

And the audience was learning to become citizens.
