Here’s a warm scene-version you can drop straight into chat — it answers “what’s the story?” by showing it.

⸻

One’s sitting in a windowless office in a federal building that smells like old carpet and burnt coffee. Not a glamorous place. Not a heroic place. Just fluorescent light and paper and the quiet hum of a machine that never sleeps.

Across the table is a senior staffer from President Carmichael’s orbit—polite voice, cold eyes—sliding a folder toward him like it’s a courtesy.

“Sign this. You’ll be protected. You’ll have access. You’ll be useful.”

One doesn’t open it right away. He already knows what it is: a loyalty agreement dressed up as “national stability.” A clean little document that turns conscience into compliance.

In the hallway outside, a TV is playing the President’s speech on loop. The crowd is cheering. The words are warm. The intent underneath them isn’t.

One finally opens the folder. The language is smooth. Legal. Almost gentle. It doesn’t say “purge,” it says “reform.” It doesn’t say “silence,” it says “unify.” It doesn’t say “enemy,” it says “threat.”

He looks up. “And if I don’t sign?”

The staffer smiles like this is all very reasonable. “Then you’ll be… difficult. And difficult people don’t last long in this city.”

That’s when One understands what his job really is. Not to win. Not to overthrow. Just to create friction—paper trails, audits, procedural delays, coalition pressure—anything that buys time and forces truth into daylight.

Later that night, Sandi finds him on the steps outside, coat pulled tight, eyes tired in the way only someone raised near power can be tired.

“You can’t fight him,” she says softly. Not as a warning. As a confession.

One doesn’t argue. He just says, “I’m not trying to destroy him. I’m trying to stop the part of him that doesn’t care who gets crushed.”

She sits beside him, close enough to feel the heat of his breath in the cold.

“And what does that make you?” she asks.

One watches the lights in the windows above them—hundreds of rooms full of people choosing comfort over conflict.

“It makes me a witness,” he says. “And if I can… a lock on the door.”

And somewhere upstairs, Carmichael is still speaking into the cameras, smiling like a savior—while the country slowly learns the difference between comfort and conscience.
