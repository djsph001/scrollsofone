Scene: The Rule

He didn’t write it like scripture.

He wrote it like a manual for citizens who didn’t want to become monsters.

Sandi sat across from him, quiet, hand on her stomach. Auntie Jo moved in the kitchen, radio low, soup simmering like a steady heartbeat.

One titled the page and spoke each line as he wrote it, testing it out loud:

FOURTH BRANCH ETHIC: CARE AND TRUTH

1) Care without capture.
If help requires your identity, your loyalty, or your silence, it is not help.
It is recruitment.

2) Truth without spectacle.
If truth requires humiliation, rage, or performance, it is not truth.
It is theater.

3) Method over charisma.
We do not follow people.
We follow tests.

4) Redundancy over centralization.
No single point of failure.
No one hero.
No one archive.
No one fund that can be seized.

5) Safety is not cowardice.
If a room is threatened, we reroute.
We do not turn risk into content.

6) Put it in writing.
Power hates paper trails.
So we demand them.

7) Protect the vulnerable from symbolization.
Children are not banners.
Victims are not marketing.
Witnesses are not disposable.

8) Never punish people for being tired.
The exhausted are the primary targets of capture.
So we build support, not shame.

One stopped writing.

He looked at the page like it was too simple to matter.

Sandi read it once, then again.

“This is the spine,” she said quietly.

One nodded.

“It has to be,” he replied. “Because the whole war is about replacing spine with feed.”

Auntie Jo stepped into the room and looked over Sandi’s shoulder.

She read the rules without expression.

Then she nodded once.

“Good,” she said. “Now you do it.”

One exhaled.

That was the real test:

Not writing ethics.

Living them.

Sandi looked at One.

“Do we publish it,” she asked.

One considered.

“If we publish it too loudly,” he said, “they’ll flood it. They’ll meme it. They’ll counterfeit it.”

Sandi’s eyes narrowed.

“So we publish it the way we publish everything,” she said.

One nodded.

“As a flyer,” he replied. “Quiet. Practical. Passed hand to hand.”

He tore the page out carefully.

Not dramatic.

Just deliberate.

Helen would print it.

Delphine would post it in the church kitchen.

The barber would tape it behind the mirror where only staff could see.

The veteran would carry it folded in his jacket like a pocket constitution.

And Keisha—tired, scared, holding her kid’s hand—would read rule #8 and understand something for the first time in weeks:

The circle wasn’t judging her.

It was built for her.

Outside, the regime kept manufacturing noise.

Inside, a new ethic was being learned the only way ethics are learned:

In rooms.

By people.

Under ordinary light.
