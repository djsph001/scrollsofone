Scroll of Carmichael III

The Stolen Election

They called him before the numbers were final.

That, more than anything, offended him.

“We’re seeing a trend, Mr. President,” the campaign manager said over speakerphone.
“Key suburbs are underperforming. Turnout shifts in the cities. The path is… very narrow.”

“Narrow isn’t closed,” Carmichael replied.

A pause.

“No, sir,” the manager said. “Not yet. But…”

He trailed off. Coward.

“But what?” Carmichael snapped.

“But the networks will probably call it within the hour,” he said.
“We need to prepare a statement.”

There it was.

The word he’d spent his entire life outrunning:

Concession.

⸻

He hung up before they could say anything else.

Staff would swarm. Advisers would knock.
Everyone would want to “guide” him through this moment
like he was some stunned widow who needed help filling out paperwork.

He walked to the window instead.

Outside, the capital glowed like nothing was wrong.
Cars moved. Streetlights held.
Somewhere, someone was opening a bottle of cheap champagne
because the numbers on their preferred channel made them feel like winners tonight.

He pressed his hand against the glass.

They think this is a swing, he thought.
They think history is a light switch. On one year, off the next.

He had bled too much for this job
to believe that a handful of districts
in states most of his voters couldn’t find on a map
had the right to end his story.

No.

Something was off.

If he couldn’t feel it in his gut,
he saw it in the data.

⸻

The internal reports had been clear:
	•	Base enthusiasm high.
	•	Opposition fractured.
	•	Economic numbers ugly but survivable if framed correctly.

He’d hit his marks at the rallies.
He’d played the assassination attempt like a violin: humble, grateful, defiant.

His people loved him more, not less.

So how did he “lose”?

He flipped through the latest precinct maps.

Pockets of turnout in places that had been lukewarm last time.
Weird spikes in absentee ballots.
Numbers that didn’t match the softness he’d felt in the room at certain town halls.

He’d always trusted crowds more than charts.

Crowds never lied to his face.

He remembered them:
the chanting, the tears, the “we love you” signs that looked homemade
even when they weren’t.

Was he supposed to believe
that all of that devotion dissolved in a few months
because a few commentators tut-tutted on air?

No, he told himself.
Something else is at work.

The alternative—that they had simply grown tired of him—
was not admissible evidence in his personal court.

So he did what he’d trained himself to do long before he ever stepped into office:

He rewrote the charge
so he could still play the hero.

⸻

The first advisor to say the word “lose” to his face
was the pollster.

Bad choice.

“Sir, the voters have sent a clear message,” the man began.
“They—”

“No,” Carmichael said, voice flat.
“They haven’t.”

The pollster blinked.

“With respect,” he tried again, “the margins—”

“The margins tell me what was counted,” Carmichael said.
“Not what was cast. Not what was suppressed. Not what was manipulated.”

He stepped closer.

“Do you believe,” he asked softly,
“that the people who chanted my name in the rain,
who drove six hours to stand in a field for three,
who told me I was the first man in years who spoke like they did—
do you believe those people just… changed their mind
because a few pundits called me dangerous?”

The pollster faltered.

“Public opinion can—”

“Public opinion is a product,” Carmichael snapped.
“They manufacture it. We’ve been manufacturing it.
Now someone else has outbid us.”

He watched the man swallow.

There it was again.
The thing he refused to say out loud:

Maybe they’re just tired of you.

He would not give that thought room to grow.

⸻

The second advisor tried a different angle.

“This could be good for your legacy,” she said.

He laughed in her face.

“Do I look like a man who wants a library?” he asked.

She flushed.

“What I mean,” she said carefully,
“is that peaceful transfer of power, gracious concession—
those are the things history looks kindly on.”

“History,” he said, “is written by people who survived long enough to own a pen.”

He poured himself a drink he didn’t need.

“If I step down,” he said, “do you think the people who put me here
will feel peace?
Or will they feel abandoned?”

She didn’t answer.

“Exactly,” he said.
“To them, this isn’t ‘the process working.’
It’s proof that the system is rigged against anyone who talks like they do.
If I validate that with a smile and a handshake,
I’m telling them their enemies were right about me.
That I’m just another actor reading lines about unity
while their towns crumble.”

He took a slow sip.

“Do you want to be the one to explain that to them?” he asked.

She looked away.

He’d won that argument.
Not because he was right,
but because he understood which fear to touch.

⸻

It didn’t take long for the stories to start.

Whispers from county officials.
Videos of “glitches” in late-night counts.
Screenshots of tallies that went up and down in ways
that looked sinister to people who lived on a diet of outrage.

Most of it was noise.

He didn’t care.

He cared that it gave him a word
he could use instead of “defeat.”

Stolen.

Stolen meant:
	•	He was still wanted.
	•	He was still chosen.
	•	The problem wasn’t him.

Stolen meant:
	•	An enemy to name,
	•	A wrong to right,
	•	A war to fight.

He could work with that.

He started small.

“We’re looking into irregularities,” he said on camera.
“Every legal vote should be counted.”

Reasonable. Presidential.

Behind closed doors, his language sharpened.

“They’re taking it from us,” he told donors and loyalists.
“They can’t beat us at the ballot box, so they’re doing it in the dark.”

He watched the phrase “in the dark” catch fire in their eyes.

Dark meant them.
Them meant everyone who’d ever made his voters feel stupid, small, or obsolete.

He whispered to that wound like a lover.

⸻

His lawyers told him the cases were weak.

He knew.

He didn’t need the courts to reverse the outcome.

He needed the fight itself
to overwrite the loss.

If enough people believed the election was illegitimate,
then the man sworn in after him
would sit on a cracked throne.

He might hold the office.
Carmichael would hold the grievance.

And in this country, grievance traveled farther than law.

⸻

The Baptist—back when he still spoke to him as a man—
had once warned him about this.

“Resentment is a sacrament now,” the Baptist had said.
“People take it like communion.
You start feeding them that as your main diet,
they’ll drink poison if you bless it.”

At the time, Carmichael had rolled his eyes.

Now, staring at the screen as crowds chanted “Stop the Steal”
in cities across the map,
he realized the Baptist had been half right.

Resentment wasn’t just a sacrament.

It was fuel.

And he had more of it in storage
than any of his rivals.

⸻

His staff drafted two speeches.

One was concession:
	•	Call for unity.
	•	Acknowledge “the will of the people.”
	•	Pledge smooth transfer, graceful exit.

The other was something else:
	•	Legal challenges.
	•	“Serious concerns.”
	•	“We owe it to our supporters to exhaust every avenue.”

He read both.

The concession speech felt like a eulogy
for someone he didn’t know.

The second felt like a to-do list.

“Use this one,” he said, tapping the page with the “concerns.”

“Sir,” the chief of staff said carefully,
“there is a real risk of violence if we push this too hard.”

“Violence from whom?” he asked.

“Your supporters,” she said. “They trust you. If you tell them—”

“If they act,” he interrupted, “it will be because they have been pushed to the edge for years.
I didn’t load that spring.
I just pointed to it.”

“That’s not how they’ll see it on the news,” she said.

He smiled thinly.

“Since when has the news ever seen me clearly?” he asked.

⸻

He went before the cameras,
the mask perfectly in place.

He did not call for calm.

He called for vigilance.

He did not bless the outcome.

He blessed the suspicion.

He didn’t explicitly say the words “stolen election.”
He didn’t have to.

He just asked, over and over:

“Does this feel right to you?”

He knew the answer in their bones would be no,
because their lives hadn’t felt right in years,
and nobody powerful had ever told them
that their discomfort counted as data.

He offered them that validation now,
at scale.

Not because he intended to fix their lives,
but because their pain was the only currency
that could buy him another chapter.

⸻

In the weeks that followed,
as courts dismissed case after case,
as fact-checkers debunked claim after claim,

he felt something harden inside him.

It wasn’t grief.
He didn’t allow himself that.

It was contempt.

For the judges who refused to “see the bigger picture.”
For the reporters who treated his supporters like a pathology.
For the colleagues who urged him in private to concede
and then went on TV to praise “our resilient democracy”
while his people filled the streets.

He told himself they weren’t just attacking him.

They were attacking the country that had chosen him.

Therefore any blow he landed in response
was an act of defense.

Self-defense.
National defense.
The lines blurred pleasantly.

⸻

He began talking about “enemies of the people” more freely.

He named departments.
Then agencies.
Then individuals.

“Not everyone who opposes me is an enemy,” he said in one interview.
“But every enemy this country faces
seems strangely aligned against me.”

He watched that line clip itself into a meme.

He didn’t care if it was logically sound.

He cared that it felt true
to millions of people
who had decided their fate and his
were braided together.

⸻

Privately, he made himself a promise:

I may have to leave the building.
But I will not leave the stage.

He would do his term,
perform the rituals,
step aside when forced to.

But only in the most technical sense.

He would not bless the man who came after him.
He would not frame this as “the will of the people.”
He would frame it as the will of the machine—
	•	The media machine,
	•	The bureaucratic machine,
	•	The voting system machine,
	•	The foreign influence machine,

all of which, he now believed with near-religious certainty,
had converged for one purpose:

To remove him from the place God and the people
had clearly intended him to occupy.

Did he have proof of that?

Not the kind a court would accept.

But he had a bullet scar and a crowd that still chanted his name.

For him, that was evidence enough.

⸻

This is the record of the season

when President Carmichael decided

that losing an election
could not mean he had been rejected,

only that destiny had been interrupted

by thieves
who now needed to be found,
named,

and punished.
