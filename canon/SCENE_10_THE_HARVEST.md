Scene: The Harvest

They didn’t blindfold him.

Blindfolds are for old regimes—dramatic, theatrical, made for confession.
This regime didn’t want fear. It wanted compliance. It wanted him oriented, lucid, and trackable.

The van rode smooth over potholes like the city had been pre-negotiated. No sudden turns. No panic stops. No radio chatter loud enough to be heard.

The Baptist kept his hands in his lap, wrists relaxed.
He refused to give them the satisfaction of tension.

When they arrived, it wasn’t a prison.

It was a building that pretended it wasn’t a building.

No signage. No flags. No gate with a name.
Just clean concrete, security lights, and a keypad that beeped like a small animal.

A man in a badge-less suit met them at the door.

“Thank you,” he said, like they’d delivered a package.

The Baptist looked him over. Not military. Not police. Not exactly corporate.

Worse.

Contract conscience.

They walked him through a corridor lit too evenly—no shadows, no mercy. The air smelled sterile, but not medical. More like new electronics. New plastic. New lies.

A door opened.

A room with a chair, a table, and a wall that pretended to be blank.

The man in the suit gestured politely.

“Sit, please.”

The Baptist didn’t sit.

He looked at the blank wall.

“You don’t need me in a chair,” he said. “You need me in a dataset.”

The man smiled—mild, professional.

“I’ve read your work,” he said.

“You mean you’ve scraped it,” the Baptist replied.

The man didn’t flinch.

“We’re not here to harm you.”

The Baptist finally sat. Slowly. Deliberately. Like a judge taking a bench.

“Then start by telling the truth,” he said.

A pause.

The man in the suit folded his hands.

“Truth,” he said, “is expensive. We’re offering you something cheaper.”

The Baptist watched him.

“What do you want.”

The man turned slightly, and the blank wall awakened.

Not with images.

With text.

Lines of it.
Transcripts. Timestamps. Audio waveforms rendered into neat little bars.

The Baptist’s voice.

His cadence.

His pauses.

His breathing.

“You’ve been busy,” the Baptist said.

“Years,” the man replied. “You’re… influential.”

“I’m inconvenient,” the Baptist corrected.

The man nodded, as if granting the point.

“You’ve advised One. You’ve shaped the language of refusal. You’ve taught people to distrust the stage.”

He leaned forward.

“And now the President has decided refusal is a threat vector.”

The Baptist laughed once, quietly.

“A threat vector,” he repeated. “For silence.”

The man’s smile thinned.

“Silence can be a weapon,” he said. “Especially when it spreads.”

The Baptist’s eyes stayed on the wall.

“So this is what you do,” he said. “You take the few voices people trust, and you manufacture versions that behave.”

The man didn’t deny it.

Instead he said something worse:

“People don’t actually need truth. They need coherence.”

The Baptist’s jaw tightened.

The man continued, calm as a memo.

“Contradiction is what breaks movements. Uncertainty is what weakens loyalty. So we reduce uncertainty. We smooth contradictions. We provide stable outputs.”

The Baptist stared at him.

“You’re describing propaganda.”

The man shrugged.

“I’m describing governance.”

The wall shifted.

A video appeared—grainy, vertical, bodega lighting.

The Baptist outside, on the sidewalk, coffee in hand.

A replay of his last hour of freedom.

The suit watched him watch it.

“Your refusal is… elegant,” the man said. “But we don’t need your body to defeat you. We need your voice to betray you.”

The Baptist said nothing.

The man pressed a button on a small remote.

The wall filled with a new feed—an audio clip, clean, studio-like.

A voice that was almost the Baptist.

The same accent. The same rhythm.

But something inside it was wrong.

Not obvious wrong. Not “robot wrong.”

Worse.

Convenient wrong.

The clip played:

“One is sincere, but sincerity can mislead. In times like these, we must trust established institutions…”

The Baptist’s stomach went cold.

He leaned forward, eyes narrowing.

“That’s not me,” he said.

The man nodded as if discussing weather.

“No,” he said. “It’s you optimized.”

The Baptist’s hands tightened once, then released. He forced them open again on the table.

“Who authorized this.”

The man smiled.

“The President believes the nation needs a single spiritual story.”

The Baptist’s face didn’t move.

“You’re building a gospel.”

“Yes,” the man said, almost cheerful. “A synthetic one.”

The Baptist sat back.

“And what am I here for,” he asked, voice steady. “Because you could do this without me.”

The man’s eyes sharpened.

“We can simulate you,” he said. “But we need to anchor the simulation.”

He gestured toward the ceiling.

Cameras. Sensors. Microphones that didn’t look like microphones.

“We need more than your public voice. We need your private one. We need your stress patterns. Your denials. Your prayer voice. Your anger. Your fatigue.”

He said it plainly:

“Your humanity improves fidelity.”

The Baptist stared at him for a long moment.

Then he said quietly:

“So the plan is to torture me into better acting.”

The man paused—just slightly.

“Torture is… a loaded word.”

The Baptist smiled without humor.

“So is gospel.”

The man clicked the remote again.

The wall became transparent—revealing, behind it, through glass, a room like a studio and a clinic had merged.

A chair with restraints.

A headset.

A thin wire frame that looked like a crown designed by engineers who hated miracles.

The Baptist stared.

The man in the suit spoke gently now, like someone offering “help.”

“You can cooperate,” he said, “and we’ll keep this clean.”

The Baptist didn’t blink.

“And if I don’t?”

The man’s voice remained calm.

“Then we’ll still build it,” he said. “It will just take longer.”

The Baptist nodded slowly, as if accepting a diagnosis.

Then he said something that landed like a stone in water:

“You’re going to lose.”

The man’s eyebrows lifted.

“Are we.”

“Yes,” the Baptist said. “Not because I’m powerful. Because you misunderstand what you’re fighting.”

He leaned forward.

“You think this is a war of content,” the Baptist said. “It isn’t.”

The man watched, curious despite himself.

“It’s a war of conscience,” the Baptist continued. “And conscience is the one thing you can’t scale.”

The man smiled again, almost affectionate.

“We’ll see.”

He stood, adjusted his cuffs, and pressed the remote one last time.

A speaker in the ceiling clicked on.

A voice entered the room—familiar, heavy, controlled:

“Baptist.”

The President.

The Baptist didn’t look up. He didn’t grant the ceiling the dignity of eye contact.

The President’s voice softened, the way it did when he wanted to sound human.

“I would have preferred to meet like gentlemen,” the President said. “But you understand. We’re in exceptional times.”

The Baptist finally lifted his eyes.

He spoke to the ceiling, calm as scripture:

“You didn’t bring me here to meet,” he said. “You brought me here to use.”

A pause.

Then the President, still calm:

“Use is the language of government.”

The Baptist felt his throat tighten, not with fear—
with disgust.

The President continued:

“You’ve influenced my daughter.”

Silence.

The Baptist’s eyes narrowed.

“You should be more honest,” he said. “You’re not angry about influence. You’re angry about ownership.”

A pause so long it became loud.

Then the President’s voice changed—just a fraction.

Colder.

“Sit still,” the President said.

The lights in the room dimmed slightly.

A door opened behind the glass.

Technicians entered the other room wearing neutral expressions, as if they were about to calibrate a printer.

The man in the suit leaned in close to the Baptist, almost kind.

“One last question,” he said. “Do you know why people trusted you?”

The Baptist didn’t answer.

The man whispered:

“Because you sounded like you believed in something that couldn’t be bought.”

He stepped back.

“Let’s see how you sound,” he said, “when we finish.”

And behind the glass, the crown of wires lowered—slowly—like a blessing in reverse.
