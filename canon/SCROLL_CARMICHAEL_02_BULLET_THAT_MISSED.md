Scroll of Carmichael II

The Bullet That Missed

They told him later it was a miracle.

He didn’t need them to.

He’d already decided that himself.

⸻

The rally was routine.
Nothing he hadn’t done a hundred times.

Flag choreography.
Pre-vetted “ordinary citizens” behind him on stage.
Crowd thick with merch and heatstroke.

He was mid-sentence when the air changed.

He didn’t hear the first shot.
He felt the pause.

The odd, animal silence that falls on a crowd
half a heartbeat before it understands it’s in danger.

Then the sound hit—a crack that didn’t belong to fireworks or speakers.

The Secret Service moved as one organism:
hand on his back, hand on his shoulder,
dragging him down behind the podium.

Someone screamed.
Someone else yelled, “Get down!” as if anyone needed the reminder.

He tasted metal and dust and his own adrenaline.

Second shot.
Third.

A woman near the front went down.
He saw that later on replay.

In the moment, all he saw was wood splintering above his head
and a sliver of sky
he was suddenly sure he might never see again.

⸻

In the armored car afterward,
his hands were shaking.

He held them together so the agents wouldn’t see.

“Are we clear?” he asked.

“For now,” the lead agent said. “Shooter’s in custody. Local PD is securing the area.”

He nodded.

“Any word on casualties?” he asked.

They exchanged a look.

“Two confirmed injured, one critical,” the agent said. “We’ll have more soon.”

He closed his eyes.

He knew what he was supposed to think:

Those poor people.

What he actually thought was:

They missed.

He saw the bullet in his mind:
the arc, the angle, the calculation.

He thought of all the things that had to go wrong for it to go right,
and all the things that had to go right for it to go wrong.

Wind.
Timing.
His own head turning a fraction of an inch to check the teleprompter.

He should have been dead.

He was not.

He focused on that.

Not the blood.
Not the woman whose name he didn’t yet know.
Not the family already being torn open by a phone call from a stranger.

Just the simple, brute fact:

I am still here.

⸻

Back at the secure location, the doctors fussed.

No bullet wounds. Just a graze along his ear,
a line of blood that looked dramatic on camera and trivial on the chart.

“You’re very lucky,” the doctor said.

Carmichael nodded.

He did not feel lucky.

He felt chosen.

⸻

That night, he watched the footage.

Over and over.

The slow-motion replay:
his head turning,
the flash in the distance,
the splinter of wood exploding inches from his face.

Every time, he thought:

There. That’s the frame where history almost ended.

He imagined the alternative timelines,
all the ways it could have gone:
	•	Bullet in the throat: instant legend, chaos.
	•	Bullet in the chest: rushed to hospital, “fight for his life” coverage.
	•	Bullet in a supporter’s body (the one that actually happened): narrative wound, martyr by proxy.

In none of those timelines was he ordinary again.

Even in survival, he felt the escalation.

He was no longer just a President.
He was a man someone had tried to erase from the story and failed.

That meant something.

He decided what.

⸻

In the days that followed, the country rallied.

Approval numbers shot up.
Enemies suddenly used softer language.
People who had called him a threat to democracy last month
now called him “Mr. President” with a catch in their throat.

He went to the hospital, cameras in tow,
held the hand of the woman who’d been shot in the crowd.

She tried to apologize.

“I’m so sorry, Mr. President,” she whispered. “I tried to duck.”

He squeezed her hand.

“No,” he said. “I’m the one who’s sorry.”

He meant it, in the small, contained way he allowed himself to mean things.

But as he walked back down the corridor,
surrounded by agents and staff,
another thought rose and pushed the apology aside:

You took a bullet meant for me.
That has to count for something.

Not against her.
Against the world.

⸻

That night, alone in the residence,
he poured himself a drink he didn’t need
and opened a Bible he hadn’t read in years.

He flipped it like a deck of cards,
letting the pages slap against his thumb
until it fell open somewhere in the prophets.

His eyes landed on the word anointed.

It was a translation artifact,
a word dragged across centuries and languages.

He seized it anyway.

Anointed.
Chosen.
Preserved.

If God did not want me here, he thought,
that bullet would have finished the job.

He wasn’t stupid.
He knew the physics.
Wind, angle, jittery trigger finger.

But he also knew how many men more righteous than him
had died in alleys, in cells, in hospital beds that smelled like mildew,
without anyone naming their death a tragedy on television.

If there was no pattern, then everything was chaos.

He had spent his life refusing that conclusion.

So he did what he always did.

He made a story to sit inside.

They missed because they were meant to.
I lived because I was supposed to.
This isn’t luck. It’s confirmation.

Confirmation of what?

He didn’t say it out loud.

He didn’t need to.

⸻

When his advisors came to him with new security protocols,
urging caution, a lower profile, fewer public events,

he surprised them.

“We’re not hiding,” he said.

“Sir, with respect, the risk—”

“The risk is the proof,” he snapped.
“They think they can scare me back into my box.
They think they can shoot their way out of the order I’m trying to build.”

He leaned forward.

“If I step back now, I’m telling them the bullet worked.
I’m not giving them that victory.”

The national address that night was somber, restrained, presidential.

He talked about unity.
Resilience.
The fragility of life.

He did not say,
“I am the man they could not kill.”

He didn’t have to.

Everyone was already thinking it.

⸻

In the weeks that followed, something subtle shifted.

He became less patient with criticism.
Less tolerant of dissent inside his own circle.

When someone opposed a policy,
he no longer heard “concern.”

He heard,
“You lived by accident, and this isn’t worth the risk you took.”

And he could not tolerate that.

If my life was worth a stranger’s blood, he thought,
then my project must be worth everything.
Otherwise I am just a man who let someone else fall in my place.

That was unacceptable.

So every new power grab, every line crossed,
every tool deployed that would have made his younger self sick,

became easier to justify.

The bullet had become his theology.

He was not a man clinging to office.

He was a survivor with a mandate.

⸻

This is the record of the moment
President Carmichael decided

that almost dying
meant he was right,

and that any world
which disagreed with him after that

was a world
that deserved to be corrected.

⸻
