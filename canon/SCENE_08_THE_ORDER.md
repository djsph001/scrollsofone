Scene: The Order

The West Wing didn’t feel like a building that night.
It felt like a throat.

Screens everywhere. Producers everywhere. Advisors leaning in, whispering, tightening the air until it tasted like metal.

The photo was on every monitor: Brooklyn sidewalk, clinic door, a hand on a stomach, a hand on a back. Grainy. Crooked. Human.
And already—too late.

The President stood with his hands behind him, watching the same three seconds loop like a punishment.

“Tell me again,” he said, calm in the way that made people nervous, “how this is trending in Brazil.”

His digital director—young enough to still believe work could save him—cleared his throat.

“Sir, it’s global. Multiple languages. The diaspora networks amplified it. Also—”

“Also what.”

“Also the statement from One. Comments disabled. Still went viral. It’s… framing him as disciplined. Refusing spectacle.”

The President didn’t turn around.
He watched the loop again.

Sandi’s face didn’t look scandalous.
It looked… private.

He felt something hot try to rise in him and he crushed it with practice. Rage was a luxury. Rage was what amateurs had.

“Disciplined,” he repeated. “That’s what we’re calling it now.”

The Chief of Staff shifted his weight.

“Sir, we can bury the story in twenty-four hours. The cycle is hungry. We feed it something else. Foreign policy. An arrest. A leak.”

“And let him keep the moral high ground?” the President asked, still not turning.

Nobody answered fast enough.

He finally faced them.

He looked like the man they knew from rallies and podiums and flags—clean suit, clean hair, the polished mask of certainty—but his eyes had the flat focus of someone doing arithmetic.

“The problem,” he said, “is not the pregnancy.”

A pause. A room inhaled.

“The problem is the meaning.”

He walked to the table and tapped the screen with one finger.

“This is a story now. And stories are governments.”

The Press Secretary opened her laptop like a shield. “We already issued the privacy statement, sir. Standard language. Family matter.”

He smiled without warmth.

“Privacy is for citizens,” he said. “Not for symbols.”

Then he turned to the political director.

“What are my people saying?”

“Outrage,” the director replied quickly. “They feel—betrayed. Hypocrisy. Borders. Family values. The whole—”

“Yes,” the President said. “Good.”

The room relaxed by a fraction. Outrage was manageable. Outrage was fuel.

But then the digital director added, too honestly:

“And also, sir… not all of them. Some are confused. There’s… sympathy. The ‘leave her alone’ angle is getting traction in places we don’t control.”

The President’s eyes narrowed.

“Who started that?”

“No one,” the director said. “It’s—organic. People… practicing restraint. It’s small but—”

The President held up his hand.

“Stop.”

He stared at the photo again and something like disgust crossed his face—not at Sandi, not at One, but at the idea of people refusing to be steered.

Restraint was dangerous.
Restraint made crowds unpredictable.
Restraint made power look unnecessary.

He leaned forward, palms flat on the table.

“Listen to me,” he said quietly. “We do not lose to silence.”

Nobody breathed.

He turned to the Counsel.

“Can I compel a public statement from my daughter.”

The Counsel chose each word like stepping stones over a river.

“Not legally, sir.”

“And not… practically,” the Chief of Staff added, gently, as if speaking to a man near a ledge. “She’s not staff. She’s family.”

The President’s jaw worked once.

“Family,” he repeated, and the word sounded like a tool he’d once owned.

“Call her,” he said.

The Press Secretary hesitated. “Sir—”

“Call. Her.”

A staffer dialed. Put it on speaker.

It rang long enough for the room to feel shame.

Then Sandi’s voice—calm, close-mic, no performance.

“What.”

The President’s smile returned, the one cameras loved.

“Sweetheart,” he said. “We need to handle this.”

A pause.

“I’m handling it,” she said.

The room froze. Even the screens seemed to dim.

“This is not the time,” he said, still smooth, “to be stubborn.”

“It’s exactly the time.”

He clenched his hands behind his back again.

“You understand what they’re doing to you?”

“You mean what you are doing?” she asked.

That landed like a slap no one could acknowledge.

He kept his tone steady.

“They’re using you to weaken me.”

“No,” she said. “They’re using me to entertain themselves. You’re the only one calling it strategy.”

His eyes narrowed, sharp.

“Your silence is being interpreted.”

“I’m okay with that.”

“You think you’re okay with it,” he corrected. “You think you’re immune. But you’re not.”

“Dad,” she said, and for a second there was something almost tender—almost. “Stop trying to own the meaning of my body.”

The President’s face didn’t move, but the temperature in the room dropped.

“You’re making a mistake,” he said.

Sandi exhaled slowly.

“You don’t get to call it a mistake just because you can’t control it.”

The Chief of Staff closed his eyes.

“Sweetheart,” the President said, voice tightening, “do you know what this does to the country.”

“It does nothing to the country,” she said. “It does something to your story.”

A pause.

Then, in a tone that was almost bored:

“I’m not your story.”

And the line went dead.

The President stared at the phone as if it had betrayed him personally.

No one moved.

One of the screens replayed One’s statement in white text on black:

That child is not a political statement…

The President looked at it, and you could see the calculation settle into place like a blade sliding back into a sheath.

“Alright,” he said softly.

He turned to the room.

“New plan.”

Everyone leaned forward, grateful for orders. Orders meant the world still had a spine.

“We do not fight him on the stage,” he said. “We fight him in the mirror.”

The digital director blinked. “Sir?”

The President’s eyes stayed on the text.

“If the public wants a prophet,” he said, “we give them one.”

The Counsel shifted. “That sounds like—”

“Like what,” the President snapped, and the Counsel shut up.

He pointed at the screen.

“He disables comments,” the President said. “So we create comments. We create responses. We create versions of him that say what we need him to say.”

The digital director’s throat bobbed.

“You mean… AI synthesis.”

“I mean reality management,” the President said. “You people love new words.”

He began pacing, energized now—not angry, not panicked—focused.

“You’re going to flood the feed with content that feels like him but isn’t him. Same cadence. Same fatigue. Same righteous little kitchen metaphors.” He waved a hand, mocking. “Same refusal energy.”

The room tightened.

The Chief of Staff tried to tread carefully.

“Sir, the risk is attribution. If it gets traced—”

“It won’t,” the President said.

The digital director spoke before fear could stop him.

“That’s… illegal, sir. Impersonation. Fraud. If it’s exposed—”

The President stopped, and the whole room stopped with him.

He looked at the director as if seeing a child who still believed rules were made for rulers.

“Do you know what the law is,” he asked, almost gently, “when you are trying to save a nation?”

The director didn’t answer.

The President leaned closer.

“The law is the story that wins.”

The Press Secretary swallowed.

“Sir,” she said, “what’s the message.”

The President looked back at the pregnancy photo.

His voice softened.

“The message,” he said, “is that One is using my daughter.”

The digital director flinched. “Sir—”

“And if he denies it,” the President continued, “we show clips of him denying it. If he refuses spectacle, we show him embracing spectacle. If he says the child is not a symbol, we show him calling the child a prophecy.”

He tapped the table.

“Contradiction breaks trust. Trust breaks movements.”

The Chief of Staff nodded slowly, understanding the mechanics even if he hated them.

“And Sandi?” the Press Secretary asked carefully.

The President didn’t look at her.

“Sandi stays silent,” he said. “We will speak for her.”

A cold wave passed through the room.

The Counsel tried again, weakly.

“Sir, we can’t—”

The President cut him off.

“We already do.”

He turned back to the digital director.

“You’re going to build me a pipeline. I want output within forty-eight hours.”

The director looked like he might vomit.

“Sir,” he whispered, “if we do that, we need a voice people already recognize as holy. Otherwise it reads like propaganda.”

The President’s eyes lit slightly.

“Exactly.”

He walked to the window. Looked out at the dark.

Then he said, almost to himself:

“Get me the Baptist.”

The Chief of Staff stiffened. “Sir?”

“The mentor,” the President said, voice calm again. “The one with credibility. The one who can bless a version of this story.”

The Press Secretary hesitated. “He’s not government.”

The President smiled.

“Nothing that matters is,” he said.

The room waited.

The President didn’t turn around.

He spoke like a man signing paperwork.

“Find him. Bring him in quietly. No headlines.”

The Counsel found a shred of courage.

“Sir… you’re describing abduction.”

The President finally turned back to them.

His expression was mild, almost patient.

“I’m describing national security,” he said.

A pause.

Then, as if offering scripture:

“Every kingdom,” he said, “has its sacrifices.”

No one responded.

The President nodded once, decision completed.

“Move,” he said.

And the room exploded into motion—phones, doors, footsteps—people relieved to be useful, terrified to be remembered.

On the wall, One’s statement kept glowing in clean white letters on black.

That child is a person.

The President watched it for one more second.

Then he said, almost conversationally:

“We’ll see.”

And somewhere, deep in the building’s throat, the machines began to sing.
