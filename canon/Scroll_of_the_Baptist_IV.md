Scroll of the Baptist IV

The Trial of Two Voices

It was 2:13 a.m.

The city outside One’s window was doing its usual impression of being alive:
sirens somewhere far off, a drunk couple arguing on the sidewalk,
a delivery truck exhaling at the corner.

Inside, the apartment felt like a held breath.

Laptop open.
Phone face-down.
The room lit only by the screen and the streetlamp slicing the blinds.

He hadn’t slept properly in three days.

Every time he closed his eyes, he saw the Baptist’s empty chair
and that too-clean video
telling him fear was not from God
and that resistance was ego with better branding.

He’d watched it ten times.
He’d watched it twice as many.
He’d stopped counting.

He reached for his phone to distract himself.

It buzzed before he touched it.

Two notifications. Almost simultaneous.
	•	Signal: Unknown number. Voice message (0:57).
	•	YouTube: “The Baptist – Official Channel: A Personal Message to a Friend”

His heart started doing double-time.

He stared at the screen.

Two messages.
Two Baptists.

Trial by algorithm.

⸻

1. The First Voice
He opened Signal first.

The app warned him: This sender is not in your contacts.

The number was foreign-looking, wrong country code,
but that meant nothing anymore.

He hit play.

The audio was rough, like it had been recorded in a stairwell
on a bad phone with worse reception.

The Baptist’s voice came through in fragments.

“—One. Listen. I don’t have long.”
Static.
“They’re—”
static again “—copying everything. Not just me. You. Your sessions. The old tapes.”
A breath.
“You were right to doubt the video. That wasn’t me. The cadence is wrong. I never—”
more static “—fear is not from God’ without telling you when fear is holy. They flattened it.”

A muffled noise, like someone moving quickly.

“I need you to do something I didn’t prepare you for.”

The sound shifted; when he spoke again, his voice was lower.

“Do not try to save me. Do you understand? That is the trap.
They want you to make this about me. About our relationship. About loyalty.”

Silence, then:

“You remember the questions?”

One felt his throat tighten.

“Use them. On me. On this. On what I’m about to say.
If it fails the test, burn this message and assume they got me.”

A door creaked in the background.

“Your assignment is simple and impossible:
Choose the path where your conscience survives, even if your reputation doesn’t.
Even if your ‘movement’ dies with it.”

Footsteps. Voices, too faint to make out.

“If you never hear from me again, assume two things are true:
One: I fought.
Two: They recorded it.”

The message cut.

0:57.

One replayed it twice.
Three times.

Every instinct in him wanted to believe it.

That gravel in the voice. The timing of the pauses.
The way he said “they flattened it” —
the Baptist always talked about flattening.

Still.
He’d just been warned not to trust himself.

He set the phone down,
hands shaking.

Then he clicked the YouTube notification.

⸻

2. The Second Voice
Same channel. Same branding.

The Baptist – Official Channel
Title: “A Personal Message to a Friend”

The thumbnail was the Baptist’s face, softer than usual.

He hit play.

“One,” the Baptist said, looking straight into the camera.
“This one is for you.”

The production was immaculate.
Warm lighting, studio mic, clean background.

“I know you’ve seen people share my last message.
I also know you’re probably angry. And scared.”

A small smile.

“You have every right to be. We live in a time of manipulation and confusion.
Voices twisted, platforms gamed, trust hacked.”

He nodded, slowly, like he was agreeing with himself.

“You’re right to be suspicious. Even of me.”

For a moment, One felt his chest loosen.

Then the Baptist continued:

“But suspicion can become a new kind of pride.
The belief that only you see clearly,
that everyone else has been captured by ‘the system’ except you and a few enlightened friends.”

He leaned closer to the camera.

“That’s how prophets turn into cult leaders.”

One flinched.

“You’ve been given a sharp mind, a sensitive conscience.
Those are gifts.
But gifts can become weapons—against yourself and others—
if you refuse to be accountable.”

He let that hang.

“You’re angry for good reasons.
You see injustice, corruption, cynical uses of faith and technology.
I see them too.”

He sighed.

“But the answer is not to burn everything down.
Not to set yourself up as a parallel moral authority.
Not to dismiss institutions wholesale because they are compromised.”

He folded his hands.

“I am asking you, as your mentor and your brother in faith:
Step back.
Pause your public work.
Submit yourself to a small circle of trusted leaders—
people with experience, with institutional responsibility—
and let them help you discern your next steps.”

His eyes softened.

“This is not surrender. This is humility.
If your calling is real, it will survive a season of hiddenness under wise counsel.
If your calling cannot handle that, then it was never from God.”

He smiled again, that same gentle smile.

“I love you.
Don’t confuse resistance with righteousness.
Sometimes, laying down your sword is the bravest thing you can do.”

The video ended.

No ads.
Comments turned off.

One stared at the blank screen.

Two Baptists.
Two assignments.

One said: Don’t try to save me. Save your conscience.
The other said: Step back and submit to institutional gatekeepers.

Both sounded plausible.
Both used his language.
Both knew his pressure points.

He opened his notes app.

⸻

3. The Five Questions
He copied the list to a fresh page.

Five Questions
(From the hospital cafeteria — Scroll II)
	1.	Who pays the price if I obey this?
	2.	Who gets safer, and who gets more exposed?
	3.	Does this command make me more honest, or more useful?
	4.	Would this still be ‘wise’ if I had no audience at all?
	5.	Is there any part of this that smells like cowardice dressed as prudence?

He put “Signal” at the top of one column, “YouTube” at the top of the other.

His pulse was loud in his ears.

He started with the video.

⸻

Question 1: Who pays the price if I obey this?
YouTube Baptist:
If he obeyed the video, he’d step back, go quiet, submit to “trusted leaders.”

Who paid?

He’d lose public momentum, sure.
Followers would drift.
Some would say he’d “come to his senses.”

The regime would look good.
Opposition would fracture.
“See? Even the firebrands calm down under proper guidance.”

The cost was mostly reputational.
High for him.
Low for everyone else.

Comfortable for power.

He wrote:

YouTube:
Me: reputation.
Them: less pressure.
Vulnerable: nothing changes.

Signal Baptist:
If he obeyed that voice—
if he refused to make it about rescuing the Baptist,
if he focused on protecting conscience over winning—

Who paid?

He might have to say things that alienated his own base.
He might refuse alliances that made him stronger but dirtier.
He might walk away from strategies that “worked” but felt like poison.

The cost landed mostly on him and his “movement.”
No neat payoff.
No heroic optics.

He wrote:

Signal:
Me: control, narrative, maybe movement.
Them (regime): less predictable opponent.
Vulnerable: maybe long-term gain, short-term chaos.

No clean answer.
But one felt tidier than it should.

⸻

Question 2: Who gets safer, and who gets more exposed?
YouTube Baptist:
If One pulled back under institutional oversight,
who got safer?

He did, a little.
Less public risk.
Less edge.

Institutions did.
They could say, “We’re handling it internally.”

The people in the blast zone of policy and propaganda?
They stayed exactly where they were.

He wrote:

YouTube:
Safer: government, institutions, me (legally, maybe).
Exposed: same folks as before.

Signal Baptist:
If One refused to play savior,
refused to let the struggle be reduced to his feud with the President,
who got safer?

He did not.
He’d stay visible.
Stay dangerous to the story they were selling.

The vulnerable might—eventually—benefit from a cleaner fight,
one not tangled up in hero worship.

But in the short term,
no one got safer.

He wrote:

Signal:
Safer: no one immediately.
Exposed: me, my people, the lie itself.

He leaned back.

Both paths carried risk.
But only one redistributed it away from power.

⸻

Question 3: Does this make me more honest, or more useful?
“Useful to whom?” he wrote under that question.

YouTube Baptist:
The message made him “useful” to systems that wanted to manage him.

If he obeyed, he could brand it as “a season of reflection,”
“submitting to elders,”
“choosing humility.”

He could keep the moral high ground publicly
while becoming harmless privately.

Honesty?

He’d be hiding the real reason:
they had scared him into obedience
by weaponizing his mentor’s face.

He wrote:

YouTube:
Honest? Not really.
Useful? Yes — to the people who want me off the board.

Signal Baptist:
Obeying that voice meant admitting he couldn’t save everyone.
Admitting he might lose.
Admitting he might never vindicate himself.

No press release.
No narrative spin.

Just decisions paid in quiet.

He wrote:

Signal:
Honest? Painfully.
Useful? Only to conscience and whoever it protects down the line.

His skin crawled.

⸻

Question 4: Would this still be ‘wise’ if I had no audience at all?
He imagined a world with no followers.
No channel.
No movement.
Just him and a few people he loved, trying to live cleanly in a dirty system.

Would the YouTube advice still be wise?

“Submit to the wise elders of the structure that’s already half-captured,
for the sake of stability”?

Without an audience, that sounded less like wisdom
and more like a polite way of saying, “Stay tame.”

He wrote:

YouTube:
Without audience: looks like negotiated surrender.

Would the Signal advice still be wise?

“Choose the path where your conscience survives,
even if your reputation doesn’t”?

Without an audience,
that was the only thing that mattered.

He wrote:

Signal:
Without audience: still the only thing that makes sense.

His chest hurt.

⸻

Question 5: Does this smell like cowardice dressed as prudence?
He didn’t write anything for a long time.

He just replayed both messages in his head.

The YouTube Baptist:

“Don’t confuse resistance with righteousness.
Sometimes, laying down your sword is the bravest thing you can do.”

Cowardice loved that line.
It could dine out on that line for years.

The Signal Baptist:

“Do not try to save me.
They want you to make this about me.
Choose the path where your conscience survives,
even if your reputation doesn’t.”

Cowardice hated that.

He finally wrote:

YouTube:
Smells like prudence sprayed over fear.

Signal:
Smells like fear named, not avoided.

He put the phone down.

The room was too quiet.

⸻

4. The Choice
He sat there, elbows on his knees,
hands steepled against his mouth.

There was no lightning bolt.
No voice from heaven.
No glowing text on the wall.

Just two recordings.
And a framework he’d been given
by a man who might already be gone.

He opened Signal.

His thumb hovered over the message.

At the bottom, the app asked:

“Do you want to enable disappearing messages for this conversation?”

He almost laughed.

He tapped Yes.
Set it to one week.

If this was fake, it was a sophisticated one.
If it was real, he wouldn’t insult the Baptist
by archiving his last words like a collectible.

Then he typed:

“I heard you.
I won’t come looking.
I’ll guard the thing you asked me to guard.”

He hit send.

Three dots appeared.
Disappeared.
Appeared again.

Then nothing.

He waited five minutes.
Ten.

No reply.

He closed the app.

⸻

Then he opened YouTube.

His cursor hovered over The Baptist – Official Channel.

He clicked the three dots.

“Don’t recommend channel.”
“Report.”

He selected Report.

A menu popped up.

He didn’t see an option for “synthetic counterfeit of my kidnapped mentor,”
so he picked “Misleading or scam.”

It was a joke with teeth and no laughter.

He confirmed.

Then he unsubscribed.

The algorithm would not be his spiritual director.

⸻

5. The Uncertain Victory
He slumped back in his chair, exhausted.

Had he chosen correctly?

He didn’t know.

It was possible the Signal message was the fake,
engineered to keep him in the fight
so he could be painted as an extremist
when the hammer finally fell.

It was possible the YouTube message was real,
his battered mentor,
forced into a studio
and trying to smuggle real counsel
through language that wouldn’t get him disappeared for good.

It was possible he’d rejected the last authentic warning
in favor of a more flattering burden.

He would not know.
Not tonight.
Maybe not ever.

But he knew this much:

One voice had offered him a managed surrender
and called it maturity.

The other had offered him a choice that might ruin him
and called it necessary.

And he remembered the line from the hospital cafeteria:

“Real me will always leave you with a choice and a cost.
Fake me will leave you with comfort and a justification.”

He had chosen the cost.

Whatever that meant.

⸻

He closed the laptop.
Turned the phone face-down again.
Lay on the bed without undressing
and stared at the ceiling
until the dark finally swallowed the edges of the room.

Somewhere between waking and sleep,
a thought rose and would not leave:

“I would rather you distrust my ghost
than submit to my counterfeit.”

For the first time since the Baptist vanished,
One did something that felt like faith:

He walked forward on a path
where he could not prove
he was right.

Only that he had refused
the easiest lie.

Thus is recorded the Trial of Two Voices.
No verdict is offered.
Only a method, and a man who dared to use it.
