Scroll of One: The Scapegoat Ledger

They don’t want me as a leader.
They want me as a sponge.

A sponge absorbs the spill so the floor looks clean.
And when the room still smells like rot, they point at the sponge and say,
“That’s where the filth is.”

This is the trick:

When a man warns you, and you ignore him, you don’t forgive him later for being right.
You accuse him of failing to stop you.

Because if you admit you were warned, you must admit you chose comfort over correction.

During the pandemic I wasn’t a virologist.
I didn’t “predict a virus.”
I predicted a world that had traded resilience for speed, redundancy for profit, truth for engagement, and trust for optics.

I wrote it plainly:
	•	Systems built for efficiency break under strain.
	•	Institutions that spend decades lying cannot borrow belief in a crisis.
	•	Emergency powers do not retire on their own.
	•	Once fear becomes a tool, someone will weaponize it.

Then the strain came.
The fear came.
The emergency became a habit.

And now Carmichael walks through doors that were left open—
not by him alone,
but by everyone who clapped when the locks were installed.

So here comes the scapegoat choreography:

First: They dismiss the warning.
“You’re dramatic. You’re paranoid. You’re always talking about collapse.”

Then: They adopt the tools the warning opposed.
“We need decisive action. No time for process. No time for consent.”

Then: When the tools become a regime, they pretend they never wanted them.
“We only did what we had to do.”

And finally: They turn to the man who warned them and ask why he didn’t save them from themselves.
“You saw it coming. Why didn’t you stop it?”

As if a warning is power.
As if a prophet is a general.
As if truth is a weapon you can swing instead of a mirror you refuse to look into.

They want my coalition, my documentation, my ability to name the pattern—
but they do not want my accusation.

They want me useful, not dangerous.

So I have stopped asking to be understood.
I have started keeping receipts.

Not for revenge.
For the moment the next generation asks the only honest question:
How did this happen?

And when they ask, I will not argue.
I will open the ledger.

March, Year One: “If we normalize emergency authority, it will be inherited by worse hands.”
They said: Stop politicizing tragedy.

June, Year Two: “If we train people to accept surveillance as care, it will become a leash.”
They said: If you have nothing to hide…

November, Year Three: “If we let truth be decided by the loudest feed, we will not recognize reality when it arrives.”
They said: You’re being negative. People need hope.

Now the same mouths call me responsible for the monster they fed.

Sandi said it clean, the way you speak to someone you love when you refuse to let them be naïve:

“They’ll use you until you’re tired, then blame you for being tired.
They’ll call you the failure so they don’t have to confess they were complicit.”

I told her: “I know.”

Because the choice is brutal:

If I fight them for credit, I waste the hours.
If I walk away, the ground is surrendered.
If I seize power to force the good, I become the thing I’m resisting.

So yes — they will try to make me the scapegoat.

Let them.

A scapegoat has one strange advantage:
when they load their sins onto your back, you finally learn the full weight of what they’ve been carrying.

And then you can write it down.

And when their story collapses,
the record will still be here.

Not to glorify me.
To rescue the truth.

Because a civilization cannot heal from a lie it refuses to name.
