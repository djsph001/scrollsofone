Scene: The Cost

It didn’t look like a crackdown.

That was the genius of it.

No riot cops. No headlines. No mass arrests.
Just a series of “unrelated” inconveniences that all pointed the same way:

Stop meeting. Stop verifying. Go back to the feed.

It started with the teacher.

She arrived at school on Thursday and found a man in a blazer waiting outside the main office with a smile that didn’t reach his eyes.

“Ms. Rivera?” he asked.

She nodded cautiously.

“I’m with the district,” he said, holding up a badge too quickly to read. “Routine compliance follow-up.”

He spoke like he was doing her a favor.

“We’ve received concerns,” he said. “Unapproved meetings on school property. Potential political activity.”

Her stomach tightened.

“It was media literacy,” she said. “A checklist. No campaigning.”

The man’s smile stayed fixed.

“Perception matters,” he replied. “And perception is our job.”

She felt heat rise in her face.

“You’re threatening me,” she said.

The man shook his head, gentle.

“Of course not,” he said. “I’m protecting you from misunderstanding.”

Then, still smiling, he slid a paper across the counter:

NOTICE OF REVIEW — PENDING

She read the words and felt the room tilt.

Not fired. Not arrested.
Just… suspended in ambiguity.

Enough to make you behave.

That same afternoon, the barber got a letter.

CITY INSPECTION — HEALTH & SAFETY VIOLATION REVIEW

His shop had passed inspections for ten years.

Now, suddenly, there were “anonymous complaints” about sanitation.

He stared at the letter, then at his clean tools lined up like soldiers, and laughed once—sharp, humorless.

“They want me broke,” he muttered.

At the VFW, the veteran’s landlord “accidentally” filed the wrong paperwork.

A lease renewal delayed.

A rent increase “required by market conditions.”

The landlord sounded apologetic on the phone, nervous.

“Look, man,” he said, “I’m getting calls. I don’t know who they are. They’re asking… weird questions.”

The veteran’s jaw tightened.

“You mean you’re getting pressured,” he said.

A pause.

The landlord didn’t deny it.

Down the block from Auntie Jo’s building, a welfare check showed up—two uniformed officers and a social worker.

Polite. Clean. Smiling.

“We’re just here because we received a concern,” the social worker said. “Nothing serious.”

Auntie Jo stood in the doorway like a wall.

“What concern,” she asked.

The social worker glanced at her clipboard.

“Anonymous report,” she said. “Claims of an unsafe environment for a pregnant woman.”

Sandi’s blood went cold.

Auntie Jo didn’t move.

“This is a home,” she said. “Not a stage. You have no business here.”

The officers shifted, uneasy. They didn’t want to be in this doorway. They had been sent, not convinced.

The social worker tried again.

“We need to see the pregnant resident,” she said, voice tight. “Just to ensure—”

Auntie Jo smiled without warmth.

“If you want to ensure something,” she said, “ensure your office stops weaponizing ‘concern.’”

One stepped into view behind her.

Calm. Present. No anger.

“We’ll cooperate with lawful process,” he said. “But we won’t participate in intimidation disguised as care.”

The social worker’s eyes flicked to him, then away.

One continued, voice steady:

“Put your request in writing. With a case number. With a supervisor name. We’ll respond.”

The social worker hesitated.

Because writing creates trails.
And trails create accountability.

“We’ll… follow up,” she said.

They left with the same politeness they arrived with.

But the message had landed.

Sandi closed the door and felt her hands shaking.

Auntie Jo put a bowl of soup on the table like it was a shield.

“Eat,” she said, flat.

Sandi swallowed. “They’re using me.”

One sat down slowly.

“They’re using the idea of you,” he said. “Because it’s cleaner than violence.”

Sandi’s voice sharpened.

“Violence is still violence,” she said.

One nodded.

“Yes,” he said quietly. “But this kind makes people feel guilty for naming it.”

A message came in from Helen the librarian—through the secure chain, plain and factual:

Three hosts hit this week. Inspections. job pressure. welfare check rumor. People scared.

Another message followed:

No one’s quitting yet. But they’re hesitating.

Sandi stared at the screen.

“This is what he meant,” she said. “Make the weather personal.”

One closed his eyes for a second.

Then opened them.

“We built method,” he said. “Now we build support.”

Sandi looked at him. “Support how.”

One’s voice stayed calm.

“We stop asking people to hold the line alone,” he said. “We build a branch that makes courage survivable.”

Auntie Jo nodded once, already understanding.

“You mean help,” she said. “Money. Food. rides. babysitting. lawyers.”

One looked at her.

“Yes,” he said. “Mutual aid. But disciplined. Quiet. Non-performative.”

Sandi’s jaw set.

“Fourth Branch isn’t a scroll,” she said. “It’s a way people don’t get abandoned.”

One nodded.

“And that,” he said, “is the part he can’t flood.”
