Scene: The First Fake

It didn’t hit like a scandal.

It hit like relief.

That was the tell.

One was alone when it arrived—late, phone-face light in a quiet room, the kind of quiet that’s never truly quiet anymore because the world keeps leaking through the glass.

A notification. Then another. Then the vibration that isn’t urgency but pileup.

BAPTIST BREAKS SILENCE.
EXCLUSIVE AUDIO.
A WORD OF CAUTION FOR ONE’S FOLLOWERS.

The clip was everywhere at once: X, TikTok, YouTube shorts, stitched into reaction videos before most people had even listened to it.

One’s thumb hovered above play.

He didn’t want to hear it.

Not because he feared criticism—
because he trusted the Baptist too much to enjoy watching the world chew him.

He pressed play.

The voice came through clean—too clean. Studio-clean.
The cadence was right. The accent was right.
Even the pauses felt like him.

For three seconds, One’s body softened.
The way it does when you hear someone you love and you think: Okay. He’s safe. He’s alive.

Then the content arrived.

“One is sincere,” the Baptist’s voice said,
“but sincerity can mislead. In times like these, we must trust established institutions. We must avoid inflaming division. The nation needs stability…”

One’s stomach dropped.

Not because it was hostile.

Because it was convenient.

It sounded like the Baptist…
edited by a committee.

It sounded like a conscience…
trained to obey.

One stopped the clip halfway through and sat there, eyes fixed on nothing.

A second clip autoplayed underneath it, same voice, same calm, same gentle correction:

“We must not romanticize disruption. We must be responsible. We must—”

One threw the phone onto the couch like it had burned him.

He stood up, paced once, then again—slow, controlled steps like he was trying to keep his mind from sprinting.

The Baptist’s first counsel came back to him like a bruise pressed in the dark:

Voices can be copied now. Faces can be worn.
Verify everything.

One leaned against the counter. Closed his eyes.

He didn’t need evidence yet.

He knew.

Not intellectually. Not analytically.

In the bone.

The Baptist didn’t speak in slogans.
He didn’t praise “institutions” without naming what they did.
He didn’t say “stability” like it was a god.

The clip was too smooth.

It had no edge. No hunger. No witness.

It was the Baptist the regime wished existed.

One picked up the phone again, thumb trembling now—not from fear, from containment.

He opened the post.

Half the comments were victory laps.

“See? Even the Baptist thinks One is dangerous.”
“Finally—someone sane.”
“Prophet exposed.”

The other half were confused, pleading, bargaining.

“Is this real?”
“Please tell me it’s fake.”
“Baptist wouldn’t say this…”

And then—worse—were the people who treated it like permission:

“Time to shut him down.”
“Arrest him.”
“Protect the country.”

One’s throat tightened.

He opened his drafts and started typing, then stopped.

He could post a denial.
It would feed the machine.

He could call it fake.
They would demand proof, and the demand would become the cage.

He could say nothing.
And silence would be interpreted as surrender.

He felt the trap assemble around him like a room.

Then his phone buzzed again.

A private message. No verified badge. No profile picture.
Just text.

VERIFY THE VOICE. DO NOT CHASE THE FEED. BUILD THE TEST.

One stared at the message.

His heart kicked once.

It read like the Baptist.
But it could also be a lure.

He exhaled slowly, forcing the air all the way out.

He opened a secure note—one of the few things he still trusted because it lived nowhere public.

He wrote three words like a vow:

BUILD THE TEST.

Then he did what he always did when the world tried to drag him onto the stage:

He turned away from the crowd…

…and called his mother.

It rang.

Marie-Claude picked up on the second ring.

“Baby?” she said, sleepy.

One swallowed hard. “Maman… listen.”

“What’s wrong?”

One’s voice stayed low.

“They took him,” One said. “They took the Baptist.”

A pause—silence that wasn’t confusion, but immediate comprehension. The kind that comes from a woman who has lived through governments that steal people.

Marie-Claude’s voice steadied.

“How you know?”

One stared at the fake clip still pulsing on the screen.

“Because they’re using his voice,” One said. “They’re putting words in his mouth.”

Marie-Claude inhaled sharply.

“Don’t answer them,” she said instantly. “Don’t fight in the street. You fight in the house.”

One closed his eyes.

“What.”

She spoke like she was tightening a knot.

“You make your truth small and strong,” she said. “You don’t make it loud and weak.”

One felt tears rise—anger tears, not sadness.

“Maman—”

“Listen,” she said. “You want to protect the child? You want to protect the girl? You want to protect yourself? Then don’t let them make you dance.”

One’s jaw clenched.

“What do I do.”

Marie-Claude didn’t hesitate.

“You do what your friend told you,” she said. “You verify.”

One opened his eyes.

The fake clip had crossed a million views.

A thousand reaction videos were being born.

The machine was eating.

And somewhere—behind glass, under lights too clean to be merciful—the real Baptist was being turned into a resource.

One looked at the screen and felt something change.

Not rage.

Not panic.

A colder clarity.

He began to type—not a denial, not a plea.

A new kind of statement.

A protocol.

A test that would force the world to choose:

Do you want truth…
or do you only want content?

And for the first time since the pregnancy photo, One understood the war in its true shape:

It wasn’t a war for followers.

It was a war for reality.
