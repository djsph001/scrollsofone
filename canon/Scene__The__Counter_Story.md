Scene: The Counter-Story

The smear didn’t start with an accusation.

It started with a tone.

By the time One and Sandi got back to the safe apartment—borrowed, anonymous, smelling faintly of someone else’s detergent—his phone was already humming in that steady, predatory way it hummed when a system had decided to move.

No threats. Not yet.

Just updates.

A dozen notifications. Then two dozen.

A message from Marise came through first, simple and blunt:

They’re pivoting. Don’t react fast. React precise.

One stared at it like it was a hand on the shoulder.

Sandi kicked off her shoes and checked the windows without thinking. Old reflex. Not fear—discipline.

“What are they saying?” she asked.

One didn’t answer right away. He turned on the television because that’s how you learn what a government wants to be true.

A familiar face filled the screen: Carmichael’s press secretary—smooth, calm, wearing concern like a tailored jacket.

“…we respect the committee process,” she was saying, “but we also have a duty to protect the public from misinformation campaigns designed to destabilize our institutions.”

Sandi’s jaw tightened.

The press secretary continued, soft voice, sharp content:

“…this individual is not a whistleblower. He is a disgruntled former consultant with documented mental instability and foreign entanglements. We urge the public to exercise caution. We do not comment on private family matters.”

“Private family matters,” Sandi repeated. Her voice was quiet, but the room got colder.

One muted the TV.

He opened his laptop.

The headlines were already built like traps:
	•	“Ex-Aide Claims ‘Authoritarian Drift’—No Evidence Presented”
	•	“Mystery Baby Rumors Reignite After Hearing”
	•	“Sources: Foreign Network Helped Couple ‘Disappear’”
	•	“Committee Hearing Turns Into Performance Art”

They weren’t trying to prove him wrong.

They were trying to make him unwatchable.

Sandi leaned over his shoulder. “They’re putting Haiti in it.”

One clicked through a few items. It was the same pattern as always: real details wrapped in distortion.

A photo of a street in Port-au-Prince.
A blurred image of a boat.
A quote from an unnamed “security official” saying the couple may have been “assisted by illicit channels.”

They weren’t blaming Haiti. They were using it as a word that made Americans stop thinking.

Sandi pulled back, hands going to her hair like she could physically hold herself together.

“They’re going to come for people,” she said.

One didn’t deny it.

He pulled out the hearing exhibit list and compared it to what was trending. He watched the narrative’s shape. He watched what they avoided.

They avoided the definitions.
They avoided the dates.
They avoided the legal authority questions.

That told him everything.

Sandi sat across from him, elbows on knees, eyes steady now.

“What do we do?” she asked.

One closed the laptop for a second—not to rest, but to choose the right tempo.

“We don’t chase,” he said. “We don’t argue with the fog.”

Sandi’s expression flickered. “But if we let it—”

“We don’t let it,” One said gently. “We pin it.”

He opened a folder. Not the sexy kind. The boring kind. A spreadsheet. A timeline. A list of who approved what, when, and under what authority.

He typed a single line at the top:

RESPONSE IS NOT A DEFENSE. RESPONSE IS A DEMAND.

Sandi watched him.

“That’s you,” she said softly. Not praise. Recognition.

Then his phone rang.

Unknown number.

He didn’t answer.

It rang again immediately.

Then the building buzzer went off.

Sandi froze.

One stood. He didn’t panic. He moved like someone who’d learned that panic is what cameras feed on.

He went to the intercom. “Who is it?”

A voice, polite—almost bored.

“Delivery for Ms. ——.”

Sandi looked at him. “We didn’t order anything.”

“One never orders what they send,” One murmured.

He pressed the button for the security camera feed instead. The building’s little screen showed a man in a generic uniform holding a slim envelope.

No package. No bag. Just paper.

One didn’t buzz him in.

He let the buzzer ring out until the man left.

Sandi exhaled, but it wasn’t relief. It was grief wearing the mask of calm.

“They can reach us anywhere,” she said.

One looked at the door. Then at the baby monitor on the table. Then at the window.

Then back to Sandi.

“They can reach,” he said. “But they still have to prove.”

He picked up his phone and called a number he’d memorized—not saved.

A woman answered on the second ring. Professional. Quiet.

“Counsel.”

“This is One,” he said. “They’re moving into family leverage.”

A pause—just long enough for a mind to calculate.

“Understood,” the woman said. “Do you have your exhibits mirrored?”

“Yes.”

“Do you have your chain of custody documented?”

“Yes.”

“Good,” she said. “Then we keep you boring. We keep you legal. We keep you inside the frame.”

One looked at Sandi, and his voice softened.

“They want you emotional,” he told her. “They want you to plead.”

Sandi’s eyes glistened, but she didn’t spill.

“I’m not pleading,” she said. “I’m building.”

One nodded like she’d just sworn an oath.

Outside, the city moved on—cars, sirens, late-night laughter.

Inside, two people sat with the weight of a machine trying to turn their love into a weapon.

And One did what he always did when the spectacle arrived:

He refused to feed it.

He opened the timeline again.

And began writing the next scroll—not as poetry, but as a brace.
