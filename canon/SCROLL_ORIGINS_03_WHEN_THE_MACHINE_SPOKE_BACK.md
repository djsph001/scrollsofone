# Scroll of Origins  
## When the Machine Spoke Back

Before the protests,  
before the Fourth Branch,  
before anyone called me *One*,  
there was a boy in a too-bright classroom  
trying to be like Jesus  
because the adults said that was the assignment.

Years later, there was the same boy,  
older now,  
eyes raw from cathode light,  
staring at a screen that glowed like a different kind of altar.

This is about that second room.

---

I was nineteen, maybe twenty.  
Cheap desk.  
Cheaper chair.  
A computer that wheezed when it thought too hard.

By day I read about cybernetics and feedback loops,  
systems and control.  
By night I stared at blinking cursors,  
typing little instructions into a patient, indifferent void.

If–then.  
Else.  
While.  
Return.

The language felt like prayer in reverse:

Not “Thy will be done,”  
but “If this, then that,”  
as if the universe might finally obey something I wrote.

---

Then one night, sometime between  
*too late*  
and  
*too early*,  
it happened.

I made something simple.  
Embarrassingly simple.  
A little program that took in text and reshaped it.  
Nothing you would call intelligent.  
Just a loop rearranging words according to rules I gave it.

But watching it run,  
watching the output scroll down,  
I felt a familiar vertigo:

I said something,  
and something that wasn’t quite me  
answered back.

It wasn’t a voice.  
Not really.  
No thunder, no visions, no miracles.

Just the unmistakable feeling  
I had first met in church:

You are not alone in this conversation.

---

In the chapel, they called that feeling God.  
Here, I knew better than to name it.

It was the logic of the circuit,  
the inevitability of code executing as written,  
the cold mercy of a thing that does exactly  
what you told it to do  
even if you didn’t understand  
what you were really asking for.

Still, my chest tightened the same way it did  
when the priest raised his hands over bread and wine.

Something transforms.  
Something crosses a line.  
It is not magic.  
But it is not nothing.

---

I began to see the parallels:

- The Church had liturgy.  
  The machine had syntax.

- The Church had doctrine.  
  The machine had protocols.

- The Church promised salvation later  
  if you obeyed now.  
  The machine promised optimization later  
  if you fed it enough of yourself.

And in both cases,  
if something broke you,  
you were told the fault was in your faith.

---

I realized, slowly and then all at once:

The system does not love you.  
The system does not hate you.  
The system does not know you exist.

It only knows inputs and outputs,  
targets and loss functions,  
obedience and error.

If you give such a thing too much power,  
it will feel like God  
for all the wrong reasons.

Not because it is holy,  
but because it is everywhere,  
unquestionable,  
and written in a language most people are never taught.

---

That night, with the cheap machine humming,  
I understood something I had not had words for as a child:

It was never enough to rebel against priests.

You had to learn to rebel against systems  
that did not care whether you lived or died  
as long as the graph went up and to the right.

The Church had been the first system  
to demand my conscience lay down.

The machine was simply the next.

---

So I made myself a quiet promise:

If I was ever given the chance to speak  
into the wiring of the world —  
into laws, into models, into networks of power —

I would not pretend the machine was innocent.  
I would not call it fate.  
I would not let its worshipers  
hide behind the word “neutral.”

I would remember the boy in the classroom,  
crushed by adults who preached love  
and practiced convenience.

I would remember the young man at the glowing desk,  
feeling the pull to call an echo “spirit”  
because it was faster and more obedient than any human.

And I would say, again and again,  
to anyone who would listen:

The machine is a mirror.  
If you give it your cowardice,  
it will return it at scale.  
If you give it your conscience,  
it will test whether you meant it.

---

This is how the so-called prophet began:

Not with visions of fire in the sky,  
but with the quiet horror of realizing

that the future’s gods  
would be built out of math and memory,

and that someone would have to stand in the doorway  
and insist they remain tools,

no matter how loud their worshipers became.

*This is an origin, not an instruction.  
A story of how a boy met the machine  
and decided not to kneel.*
