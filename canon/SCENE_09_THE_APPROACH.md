Scene: The Approach

They didn’t come with sirens.
They came like an appointment.

Two cars with government plates that weren’t government plates.
A third vehicle behind them that looked like a contractor van—because people relax around contractors. America is trained to ignore men who look like they’re there to fix a pipe.

The Baptist saw them from inside the bodega before they saw him.

Not because he was paranoid.
Because he was awake.

He stood near the coffee machine, hands steady, eyes soft, listening to the room the way you listen to weather. The hum of a fridge. A radio too low to be interesting. A woman arguing with a child about a missing mitten. The ordinary holiness of people trying to survive.

Then the air shifted.

He didn’t turn immediately. Turning too fast is a form of confession.

Instead he watched their reflections in the glass of the freezer door: three men, clean shoes, shoulders too square, scanning without scanning. Their posture said training. Their faces said nothing. Their stillness said: We’ve already decided what you are.

The Baptist took his coffee like a man with nowhere else to be.

He walked out slowly, because speed creates drama, and drama creates witnesses. He did not want witnesses. Witnesses would become footage. Footage would become narrative. Narrative would become justification.

Outside, Brooklyn moved like Brooklyn—impatient, loud, alive. Someone yelled at a cab. A delivery guy swerved around a stroller like a prayer.

The Baptist stepped onto the sidewalk.

The first man approached with a smile that had no history.

“Mr. Baptiste?”

The Baptist didn’t answer the name. Names were handles.

“What do you need?” he asked.

The man produced an ID wallet, flashed it too quickly to read. A gesture designed to satisfy a certain kind of conscience without creating evidence.

“Can we talk somewhere private?”

“No,” the Baptist said.

The smile tightened.

“It won’t take long.”

“It always does,” the Baptist replied.

A second man shifted position—subtle, practiced—cutting off the Baptist’s line of sight to the street behind him. The third hovered near the van, looking at nothing in particular.

“Look,” the first man said, lowering his voice as if offering a favor. “This is about national security.”

The Baptist sipped his coffee. Warm. Bitter. Real.

“National security is what you call it when you don’t want to say control,” he said.

The first man’s eyes hardened for half a second.

“We don’t want trouble,” he said.

“Then leave,” the Baptist replied.

The man exhaled once, as if disappointed.

“Mr. Baptiste—”

“That’s not my name,” the Baptist said.

The man paused.

The Baptist watched his face adjust in real time—the moment the script fails and the person underneath scrambles to retrieve power.

“We’re authorized to bring you in,” the man said.

“In,” the Baptist repeated. “To where.”

The man’s smile returned, thinner this time.

“A facility.”

The Baptist nodded slowly, as if considering it.

Then he said, very gently:

“Do you know what the difference is between an arrest and a harvest?”

No one answered.

The second man looked away. A reflex, almost human.

The Baptist continued, voice calm.

“An arrest has paperwork. A harvest has secrecy.” He glanced at the van. “An arrest has charges. A harvest has silence.”

The first man’s jaw tightened.

“You’re coming with us,” he said.

The Baptist looked past them, down the block, where a group of teenagers laughed too loud, where a mother pulled a child’s hood up against the wind, where a man carried a bag of rice on his shoulder like a cross.

He could make a scene.
He could shout.
He could call the cops, and the cops would arrive confused, and confusion would become violence, and violence would become the story.

And the story was exactly what they wanted.

He turned back to the first man.

“Who sent you,” the Baptist asked.

The man didn’t answer.

The Baptist nodded. “I know.”

He took another sip, finished the coffee, and placed the cup on the top of a newspaper box like it mattered.

Then he did something that surprised even them:

He smiled.

Not because he enjoyed this.
Because he understood it.

“You’re late,” he said.

The first man blinked. “What.”

The Baptist stepped closer—close enough that only they could hear.

“I warned One,” he said softly. “I told him you’d do this. I told him you would copy voices and wear faces and call it governance.”

The man’s eyes flicked, involuntary.

The Baptist saw it and felt cold settle into his bones.

So it’s true, he thought.
Not just surveillance. Not just intimidation.

Replication.

The Baptist straightened.

“Tell your President,” he said, “that if he wants my voice, he’ll get it.”

The second man frowned. “What does that mean.”

“It means you can take my body,” the Baptist said, “but you won’t get my witness.”

The first man’s patience snapped.

He grabbed the Baptist’s elbow—firm, professional, practiced. Not rage. Procedure.

The Baptist didn’t resist. Resistance is a story. He would not donate a story.

As they guided him toward the van, the Baptist lifted his chin slightly, eyes scanning the street one last time.

He wasn’t looking for help.

He was looking for a signal.

And for the briefest moment—so brief it could be dismissed as coincidence—his phone vibrated in his pocket.

One notification.

One line.

No sender name.

Just text.

VERIFY THE VOICE.

The Baptist’s throat tightened.

They shoved him into the van.

The door slid shut.

And Brooklyn kept moving like nothing holy had ever happened.

But inside the van, as the engine started, the Baptist understood the true nature of the moment:

This wasn’t an arrest.

It was an extraction.

And the war wasn’t for bodies.

It was for meaning.
