Scene: The Haiti Term Sheet

The invite didn’t come as a threat.

That’s how you knew it was dangerous.

It came dressed as respect.

A White House envelope. A UN seal. A line that sounded like an olive branch:

“National–International Resilience Initiative: Haiti Corridor.”
Requested Presence: One.

No mention of witness circles.
No mention of “analog cells.”
No mention of extremism.

Just Haiti.

A country that could swallow your whole soul if you loved it even a little.

One read the memo twice and didn’t speak.

Sandi watched his face.

“You’re thinking it’s a trap,” she said.

“It is,” One replied. “But it’s also an opening.”

Helen leaned forward. “Say the difference.”

One didn’t posture. He didn’t sermon.

He spoke like a negotiator who’d learned that moral language is useless without leverage.

“They want to use Haiti to wash their hands,” he said. “But Haiti needs speed. Real speed. So we do what politicians do: we take the meeting—and we bring paper.”

Miguel nodded slowly. “Term sheet.”

“One page,” One said. “Short enough to hold. Sharp enough to cut.”

Sandi’s hand rested on her stomach. “And no baby. No family narrative. No redemption arc.”

Helen added: “And no data capture. No ‘community stability’ enrollment disguised as aid.”

The veteran grunted. “And redundancy. If they cut one channel, the work continues.”

One looked around the table.

“This is not cowardice,” he said. “This is a deal with constraints.”

⸻

They didn’t go to the Oval.

They went to a neutral room—UN annex, glass walls, too much air conditioning. The kind of place designed to make human beings speak like brochures.

Carmichael entered with the calm of a man who believed history belonged to him.

He smiled at One like a camera was already rolling.

“Mr. One,” he said. “I’m glad you came. The world is watching.”

One didn’t smile back.

“The world is hungry,” he said. “Let’s not confuse that with watching.”

A flicker—tiny—crossed Carmichael’s face. Annoyance. Then control.

He gestured to a seat. “We’re doing something historic for Haiti.”

One sat. Put a folder on the table.

“Before historic,” One said, “we do specific.”

Carmichael glanced at the folder. “What’s that?”

“One page,” One said. “Terms.”

Carmichael chuckled, soft. “You think you’re negotiating with the United States government?”

One met his eyes.

“I’m negotiating with reality,” he said. “Your office can either help, or it can brand. Haiti doesn’t need branding.”

Carmichael’s smile tightened. “You’re here because you want results.”

“Yes,” One said. “And results require constraints.”

Carmichael nodded toward his advisor. “Let’s see your demands.”

One slid the page forward.

It wasn’t dramatic. It wasn’t poetic.

It was clean.

HAITI CORRIDOR — TERM SHEET (DRAFT)

1) Funds and contracts are public by default.
	•	published allocations, vendors, deliverables, timelines
	•	no black-box routing through “outreach” vendors

2) Independent oversight.
	•	joint audit panel: Haitian civil society + diaspora reps + neutral auditors
	•	monthly public report (written, not performative)

3) Local implementation first.
	•	minimum % of contracts to Haitian firms/NGOs where feasible
	•	diaspora logistics allowed but not dominant

4) No enrollment, no surveillance.
	•	aid access cannot require political affiliation, social media handles, or “stability program” signup

5) Proof standard.
	•	all claims must include verifiable deliverables: receipts, photos, site logs, third-party confirmation
	•	no “trust us” press releases

6) Public appearance is bounded.
	•	one joint announcement, no rally, no applause choreography
	•	no personal endorsements, no mention of the child, no “unity” branding

7) Kill switch.
	•	if capture tactics appear (data harvesting, intimidation, propaganda substitution), One withdraws publicly with documentation.

Carmichael read it silently.

Too silently.

One recognized the trick: let the other man talk himself into self-doubt.

Carmichael finally looked up.

“Some of this is reasonable,” he said.

“That’s the point,” One replied. “Reasonable is what survives.”

Carmichael tapped clause six with a finger.

“One joint announcement,” he repeated. “No rally.”

One nodded. “One bounded moment. Speech as receipt, not worship.”

Carmichael leaned back. “You don’t like crowds.”

“I don’t like staged crowds,” One said. “And you don’t like unstageable truth.”

Carmichael’s eyes narrowed.

“You’re trying to limit my messaging,” he said.

“I’m trying to keep Haiti from becoming your soap,” One replied.

A beat.

Carmichael’s advisor leaned in, whispering about feasibility, optics, control.

Carmichael waved him off.

“You’ve learned something,” he said to One, almost approving. “You’ve learned politics.”

One didn’t accept the compliment.

“I learned consequences,” he said. “People die when adults use crises as content.”

Carmichael smiled again—cooler now.

“You think this makes you safe?” he asked. “Walking into my world with a piece of paper.”

One’s voice stayed even.

“No,” he said. “It makes Haiti safer.”

Another beat.

Carmichael looked at the term sheet again, then slid it to his legal counsel.

“Draft this into an MOU,” he said. “But we adjust.”

One nodded. “You can adjust language. You cannot adjust spine.”

Carmichael gave a quiet laugh.

“One more thing,” he said. “I want you beside me at the announcement.”

One held his gaze.

“One announcement,” he repeated. “No endorsement. I speak to the deliverables, not your virtue.”

Carmichael’s smile thinned. “You’re hard to use.”

“That’s why I’m here,” One said.

⸻

Outside the room, Sandi waited with Helen and Miguel.

One came out looking tired but clean—like a man who’d held a line without pretending the line was holy.

Sandi searched his face.

“Did you give him anything?” she asked.

“I gave him constraints,” One said.

Helen’s eyes sharpened. “And he accepted?”

“He accepted paper,” One said. “Now we see if he honors it.”

Miguel nodded. “And if he doesn’t, we have the kill switch.”

Sandi exhaled. “The announcement is still spectacle.”

One didn’t deny it.

“Yes,” he said. “But bounded spectacle. Timed. For outcome.”

He looked at Sandi.

“Haiti is not my stage,” he said. “It’s my responsibility.”

Sandi’s jaw tightened—approval mixed with fear.

“And if he tries to turn it into a redemption show?”

One’s answer came fast.

“Then I leave on record,” he said. “With receipts. No yelling. Just documentation.”

Helen nodded once.

“That’s politician,” she said.

One corrected her, gentle.

“That’s citizen with leverage,” he said.

⸻

That night, One wrote a private note—one sentence—then folded it and put it in his pocket like a reminder.

Spectacle is permitted when it serves protection, never when it replaces proof.

Sandi watched him.

“This is you being a politician,” she said.

One nodded.

“Yes,” he said. “And this is me refusing to become their kind.”
