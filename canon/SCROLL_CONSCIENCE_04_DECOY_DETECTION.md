Scroll of Conscience: On Decoy Rooms
(A Checklist for Citizens in the Age of Staged Truth)

They will not always silence the truth.

Sometimes they will stage it.

They will build a fake “safehouse.”
A fake “archive.”
A fake “verification cell.”

Not to convince you it is real.

To make you feel dirty for wanting proof.

So here is a calm checklist.

Not for believers.
For citizens.

THE DECOY DETECTION CHECKLIST

1) Too Photogenic = Suspicious

Real safety is ugly.
Real discipline is boring.

If the “truth room” looks designed for a documentary—candles, dramatic posters, perfect camera angles—assume theater.

2) Too On-the-Nose = Suspicious

If you see a binder labeled “THE PROTOCOL” in thick marker, ask:

Who benefits from making verification look like a cult?

Real protocols don’t advertise themselves.

3) No Source Chain = Not Evidence

If a “raid video” appears, demand:
	•	Who filmed it?
	•	When?
	•	Where is the unedited original?
	•	What is the chain of custody?

If the chain is missing, the clip is not truth—it is persuasion.

4) Watch for Shame Hooks

Decoys are built to create one feeling:

“If I verify, I’m one of those people.”

That is the point.

Shame is the leash.

5) Check the Texture

Truth contains waste: irrelevant details, awkward pauses, ordinary life.

Propaganda is smooth: it deletes texture to improve obedience.

6) Follow the Money, Not the Mood

Decoys cost money: rentals, props, logistics.

Ask:
	•	Who paid?
	•	Who had access?
	•	Who benefits from the exposure?

7) Real Discipline Doesn’t Demand Loyalty

Verification is not worship.

If a “truth cell” asks you to cut off family, isolate from friends, or treat outsiders as enemies—

That is not verification.

That is control.

8) Boring Is a Feature

A real archive looks like this:
	•	files
	•	dates
	•	hashes
	•	timestamps
	•	redundancy
	•	restraint

No drama.

No brand.

THE POINT

Verification is not a cult.

It is hygiene.

In this age, they will try to make hygiene look insane.

Do not be moved by their theater.

Do not be shamed out of your own eyes.

Don’t chase the feed. Build the test.
And keep your truth plain—
in the kitchen light.

SCENE_29_THE_BAPTIST_SENDS_A_SIGN.md

Scene: The Sign

They underestimated boredom.

That’s what kept the Baptist alive.

Not heroism. Not rage.
Boredom—the ability to sit inside a cage without giving the captors a story.

Days blurred into procedures.

Lights on. Lights off.
Meals that tasted like compliance.
Questions asked by people who pretended to care.

“Do you feel safe?”
“Do you feel coerced?”
“Do you understand the importance of stability?”

He answered with the same calm sentence every time:

“I understand what you are doing.”

They tried flattery.

“You could help guide the nation,” the suit said one day.

The Baptist stared at him.

“You mean I could help guide your narrative,” he replied.

They tried fear.

“People get hurt,” the suit said.

The Baptist nodded once.

“People get hurt when liars need proof,” he said.

They tried exhaustion.

But boredom was stronger than exhaustion.

Because boredom had a secret:

It kept his mind free.

One night—late—an intern came to adjust equipment in the studio room. Young, nervous, avoiding eye contact like the building itself was watching her.

She dropped a pen.

The Baptist saw her hands shake when she picked it up.

He said softly, not unkind:

“You don’t like this.”

The intern froze.

Her eyes flicked up, then away.

“You shouldn’t talk to me,” she whispered.

The Baptist waited.

The intern swallowed.

“They said you’re dangerous,” she said.

The Baptist nodded.

“They say that about anyone who asks for proof,” he replied.

The intern’s throat moved. “They said you were free.”

The Baptist looked at her carefully.

“If I were free,” he said, “would you be whispering?”

The intern’s eyes watered. Just a little.

He didn’t push. He didn’t preach.

He asked one question, calm as a door opening:

“Do you know what a checksum is,” he said.

She blinked. “A… hash?”

The Baptist nodded.

“Good,” he said. “Then you understand why I need something you can’t edit.”

The intern stared at him, breathing shallow.

“What,” she whispered.

The Baptist leaned forward slightly.

“Tomorrow,” he said, “they’ll record another ‘proof’ clip. They will cut what they don’t like. That’s how they live.”

He paused.

“So I’m going to give you an ordinary detail,” he said. “A detail so strange and specific that it can’t be guessed, only witnessed.”

The intern’s eyes widened.

“And if that detail disappears,” he said, “people will know.”

She swallowed hard.

“I can’t—”

“I’m not asking you to fight them,” the Baptist said quietly. “I’m asking you to do one thing that still makes you human.”

The intern’s lips trembled.

“What.”

The Baptist spoke with deliberate slowness, like he was carving a mark into stone.

“During the recording,” he said, “I will lift my left hand. I will tap my ring finger three times.”

The intern frowned.

“That’s—”

“A signal,” the Baptist said. “Not a phrase. Not a word they can steal.”

He watched her.

“And after the recording,” he continued, “you will upload the raw file—just once—to an address I will give you. An archive address. One that does not monetize truth.”

The intern stared at him like he’d handed her a knife.

“I’ll get caught,” she whispered.

The Baptist nodded.

“Yes,” he said. “That’s why you will do it like a ghost.”

He leaned back.

“And if you can’t upload the raw,” he added gently, “then at least remember this: left hand, ring finger, three taps.”

The intern’s eyes glistened.

“Why me,” she whispered.

The Baptist’s voice stayed calm.

“Because you’re still young enough to feel sick,” he said. “That means your conscience is still alive.”

A long silence.

Then the intern gave a tiny nod.

Not brave.

Just real.

The next day, they rolled camera again.

The suit watched from off-frame like always.

“Keep it clean,” he warned.

The Baptist looked into the lens.

“My name is Baptiste,” he said. “And I am alive.”

Halfway through, as he spoke about verification, he lifted his left hand—casual, as if adjusting his cuff.

And he tapped his ring finger three times.

Once.
Twice.
Three.

So subtle most people would miss it.

But the careful people—now trained—would watch.

The suit didn’t notice.

He was listening for words, not bodies.

After the recording, as the technicians moved around, the intern’s hand hovered near the console.

Her heart hammered.

She slipped a USB into a port like she was simply doing her job.

Copy.
Eject.
Hide.

A tiny act.

Not rebellion with fireworks.

Rebellion with file transfer.

That night, somewhere far from the facility, a librarian’s secure inbox received a file.

Subject line:

RAW — PROOF

No message.

Just an attachment.

And in a room lit by a soft lamp, One stared at the clip frame by frame until he saw it—

the ring finger taps—

and he whispered, like a man hearing a friend through a wall:

“He’s alive.”

Sandi exhaled, eyes wet.

The Haitian auntie crossed herself without asking permission.

And in that moment, the regime’s greatest weakness revealed itself again:

It could counterfeit voices.

But it could not fully counterfeit witness.
