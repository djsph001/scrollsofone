Scroll of Origins XI

The Boy Who Believed

Before they called him dangerous,
before they called him arrogant,
before they called him prophet,
there was just a boy in a too-big uniform
sitting under a crucifix he took too seriously.

The nuns said,
“You must try to be like Jesus.”
They meant it as morale,
as routine instruction,
the kind of thing you say to keep children soft and manageable.

He didn’t hear it that way.

He heard an assignment.

He heard it like a contract written in chalk and blood:
If that is the highest thing a man can be,
then what else is there to aim for?

So while other boys thought about games and girlfriends and getting out,
he sat in the back row of religion class,
staring at the Stations of the Cross
and quietly asking himself
where exactly a person was supposed to put their fear
if they planned to live like that.

He was not the best student.
He was not the purest heart.
He was not even especially obedient.

He just believed them.

That was his first mistake.
	•	

Catholic school is its own small country.

The flag is a crucifix.
The anthem is guilt.
The border guards wear black and white and carry rosaries
like keys to a kingdom you’re never quite sure you deserve.

He watched how it worked.

How the rules bent for some families and not others.
How the “least of these” got quoted from the pulpit
and ignored in the hallway.
How a God who supposedly washed feet
was represented by men who never bent down.

Something in him started taking notes long before he had language for it.

He didn’t know the word “hypocrisy” yet.
He just knew that the Jesus in the stories
did not behave like the Jesus in the building.

And if he was supposed to be like Jesus,
which Jesus did they mean?

The gentle one in the paintings,
lit by candles and lilies?
Or the one who flipped tables
and stared power in the eye with no backup?

The boy felt both living in his chest
and learned early that the second one
made adults very uncomfortable.
	•	

There was a day — he will remember it until he dies —
when a teacher, tired and impatient,
humiliated a poorer student in front of the whole class
for not having the right shoes.

Afterward, they prayed aloud for “the poor.”

The boy’s stomach burned.

He didn’t have the words,
but his body knew something was wrong with that prayer.

He wondered if God accepted it,
or sent it back marked insufficient honesty.

That night, lying in a hot, dark room,
he whispered into the ceiling:

“If You’re real,
don’t let me become like that.
If I’m supposed to be like You,
don’t let them be the ones who define You.”

It was a child’s theology,
but it was clean.

He was not asking to be powerful.
He was asking not to be a liar.

That was the beginning of his allergy to lying:
the itch in his soul every time someone used holy words
to decorate an unholy act.

He would never grow out of it.
It would cost him almost everything.
	•	

They taught him that Jesus was both God and man.

No one prepared him for what it meant
to try to be “like Jesus” when you are only man.

He carried it like a private dare.

When he lost his temper,
he counted it as failure.
When he lied to avoid trouble,
he felt he had betrayed a contract no one else remembered signing.
When he looked at the world’s cruelty and felt nothing,
he worried his heart was hardening
into the same stone he saw in the eyes of the pious.

There were no medals for this.
No gold stars for excessive conscience.

Just a growing, quiet distance between him
and people who could say the creed on Sunday
and then go back to normal on Monday
as if they had not just promised everything.

He did not know it yet,
but he was already on his way out of religion
and deeper into God.
	•	

Later, much later,
they would say he was obsessed,
grandiose,
delusional.

They would say he thought he was the Son of God.

That was never it.

He never thought he was Jesus.

He thought the adults meant what they said
when they told a little boy
that the highest thing a man could be
was to live like Him.

He took that assignment seriously,
and the world never forgave him for it.

Because a boy who believes he must live like that
grows into a man who will not sit quietly
while power lies,
while churches bless what should be condemned,
while nations crucify and call it order.

They will call that madness.
They will call that rebellion.
They will call that heresy.

He still calls it
doing the homework they gave him in third grade.
	•	

If you want to understand the prophet,
don’t start with the speeches
or the manifestos
or the presidential enemies list.

Start with the child in the too-big uniform,
staring at a wooden man nailed to a wall,
thinking:

“If that’s the standard,
then half of you are lying
and the other half are asleep.

And if no one else is going to take this seriously,
maybe I have to.”

The boy did not know
that this vow would one day drag him
into politics,
into exile,
into love he could not keep,
into wars he did not want.

He only knew this:

If you tell a child
to build their life around a story,
you should be very careful
what story you hand them.

Some of them will actually do it.

And they will come back one day,
grown and furious and heartbroken,
asking why the adults never meant it.

This is his answer.

This is where it began.

The boy who believed
is still here.

He just has fewer illusions
about the people who taught him how.
