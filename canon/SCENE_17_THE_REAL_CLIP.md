Scene: The Real Clip

They gave him a chair and a camera like it was a favor.

A ring light. A neutral backdrop. A microphone clipped carefully to his collar, as if they were filming something respectable.

The man in the suit stood just off-frame, arms folded, watching with the calm attention of someone supervising a product shoot.

“Sixty seconds,” he said. “Direct. Reassuring. No drama.”

The Baptist looked into the lens.

The lens looked back like an eye that never forgave.

A technician raised two fingers. Then one.

“Rolling.”

The Baptist didn’t clear his throat.
He didn’t perform humility.
He didn’t soften himself to be palatable.

He spoke like a witness.

“My name is Baptiste,” he said calmly. “And I am alive.”

The man in the suit relaxed by a fraction.

“Today,” the Baptist continued, “there are recordings circulating that claim to be my voice.”

The suit’s eyes narrowed slightly, but he didn’t interrupt. It still sounded compliant.

The Baptist kept going.

“I cannot tell you what to believe,” he said. “But I can tell you this: verification is not paranoia. It is citizenship.”

The man in the suit shifted.

The Baptist’s tone stayed even—gentle enough to pass inspection, sharp enough to cut.

“If anyone tells you not to verify,” he said, “ask yourself why they want you unarmed.”

A pause.

The man in the suit made a small gesture to warn him. The Baptist ignored it.

“I have one request,” the Baptist said. “Do not weaponize fear. Do not weaponize love. And do not weaponize a child.”

The suit went still.

The Baptist leaned in slightly—barely, but enough to change the rhythm.

“And now,” he said, “I will do what I have always done.”

He held the camera’s gaze.

“I will name something ordinary.”

The man in the suit frowned.

The Baptist continued:

“On the day I first met One, the coffee in the church basement tasted like pennies. Bitter and brave. He drank it anyway.”

The suit’s brow tightened. Too specific. Too… lived.

The Baptist didn’t speed up.

“There was a hymn book on the table,” he said. “The cover was torn. Someone had written a phone number inside it, and the ink had bled from rain.”

The man in the suit stared, realizing what was happening too late.

The Baptist spoke with the calm precision of a man placing a coin in a locked box.

“If this clip appears later with those details missing,” he said, “you will know it was edited. And if it appears with those details replaced by something smoother, you will know it was manufactured.”

A technician’s hand hovered near the stop button.

The suit’s voice came in, low and urgent, off-camera.

“Cut.”

The Baptist didn’t stop speaking.

“And if you are hearing my voice,” he said, “and it sounds designed to give you permission—permission to hate, permission to obey without thinking—then it is not my witness.”

The suit stepped closer, now visibly tense.

The Baptist landed the final line, quiet but unmovable:

“Truth is not convenient. That is how you recognize it.”

“Stop recording,” the suit snapped.

The technician stopped.

The light went from warm to harsh in a second—not because the bulb changed, but because the room’s intention did.

The suit stepped into frame for the first time, forgetting the camera had been a theater and not a tool.

He snatched the tablet from the technician, replayed the last seconds, jaw tightening with each “ordinary detail.”

“You think you’re clever,” he said.

The Baptist sat back.

“I think you’re predictable,” he replied.

The suit stared at him, calculating.

If they cut the clip, it proved the Baptist’s point.
If they released it, it seeded a test they didn’t control.

For the first time, the suit looked less like authority and more like management confronted by a living variable.

“Run it,” the Baptist said softly, almost kindly. “If you believe your own story, let daylight touch it.”

The suit didn’t answer.

But in the corridor beyond the studio room, phones were already ringing, and the empire’s favorite fear had returned:

What if people learn to verify?
