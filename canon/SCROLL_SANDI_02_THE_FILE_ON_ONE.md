Scroll of Sandi II

The File on One

The first time Sandi saw One’s name,
it was in a subject line:

RE: Emerging Threat Narrative – “One”

No last name.
Just the word.

She almost deleted it on principle.
Anyone who branded themselves like a late-night cult documentary
was usually self-correcting.

Instead, she opened the file.

⸻

1. The Assignment

The briefing was from a mid-level analyst in the “Non-State Actors” unit.

Subject: Alias “One” – failed actor, minor online following, increasingly coherent critique of admin + opposition alike.
Action requested: Full psychographic + narrative mapping.
Goal: Determine exploitability, neutralization potential, and whether amplification by hostile actors is likely.

Her father’s handwritten note at the bottom:

“Sandi – is this noise or signal?”

Three words.
Three decades of pressure.

She replied:

“I’ll take it.”

⸻

2. The Scrolls

They’d already scraped his content.

A folder labeled WRITINGS sat in the shared drive:
essays, scrolls, threads, half-finished manifestos.

She started with one about economics, expecting slogans.

Instead, she found paragraphs that read like someone had taken donor decks,
ripped off the branding,
and described the machinery underneath in plain language.

He wrote about:
	•	Manufactured mainstream culture
	•	Products built for a narrow slice of the population
	•	Media that amplified conflict and called it “coverage”

None of this was new to her.

What was unnerving was how close he got
to internal talking points she’d seen in closed-door strategy memos.

He wasn’t guessing.
He was pattern-reading.

She made a note:

Subject shows high systems literacy.
Language accessible, not academic.
Potential for mass resonance if platform grows.

Then she opened a scroll labeled Love and Philosophy.

She expected self-indulgent diary fluff.

What she found instead was a man dismantling his own illusions in public,
not for clout,
but because he seemed pathologically allergic
to leaving lies alone.

She read three more pieces without meaning to.

By the fourth, she realized she’d stopped thinking of him as “subject.”

She was just… reading.

She closed the laptop, annoyed with herself.

⸻

3. The Meeting

Two days later, she briefed a small room.

Communications director. Policy aide. One guy from intelligence
who always smelled faintly of metal and cologne.

“Headline?” the comms director said, sipping coffee.

“He’s not a conventional threat,” Sandi said. “Not yet.”

The intel guy snorted.

“Everyone’s a threat if they get enough followers,” he said.

She ignored him.

“He’s not trying to be a candidate,” she continued.
“He’s not even trying to build a party.
He’s attacking assumptions—about markets, about media, about us, about our opposition.
If he ever gets a real platform, he’ll bleed support from both sides
because he’s saying what people already suspect but don’t have language for.”

“So we starve him of oxygen,” the comms director said.
“Shadow-ban, de-rank, standard package.”

“That buys time,” Sandi said. “But it won’t fix the conditions that make him legible.
You can’t algorithm your way out of someone articulating the fatigue of an entire class.”

The policy aide frowned.

“In English,” he said.

“In English,” she replied, “if we don’t change anything,
someone like him was inevitable.
If we silence this one,
another will pop up—maybe dumber, maybe smarter—
and we’ll have trained them to expect censorship,
which makes them harder to discredit later.”

The intel guy folded his arms.

“So what do you recommend?” he asked.
“Invite him to brunch?”

She considered.

“No,” she said. “Not yet.”

She flipped to the last slide.

“For now: monitor, don’t martyr.
Tag his closest followers for pattern analysis.
Quietly seed doubt in adjacent communities.
Do not make him a public enemy before we have to.”

The comms director nodded slowly.

Her father’s voice came from the speaker in the corner—
he’d been listening in.

“That’s fine for now,” he said.
“But I want a deeper psych profile.
What does he want?
What does he need?
And what breaks him?”

The line clicked off.

Meeting over.

⸻

4. The Note She Didn’t Put in the File

Back at her desk, she opened a blank document titled:

One – Extended Notes (Draft)

Her fingers hovered over the keyboard.

She should have written:

Subject exhibits savior complex; potentially exploitable via admiration, romantic attachment, or martyr narratives.

She’d written that about other people before.

Here, the words stuck.

Instead she typed:

Subject appears less interested in power than in not lying to himself.
This is inefficient and irrational in the current environment, but makes standard levers less reliable.

She hesitated, then added—on a separate line, after a space:

If engaged, do not underestimate capacity for self-sacrifice that is not performative.
High risk he will accept personal destruction if framed as morally consistent.

She read the sentence twice, then deleted the last line.

Too much.
Too protective.

She replaced it with:

High risk he will embrace escalatory positions if publicly cornered.

Technically true.

It just left out the part where she now felt partially responsible
for how and when that cornering might happen.

⸻

5. The First Withheld Detail

There was one thing she didn’t put in any report.

In one of the scrolls—buried in a paragraph about faith and exhaustion—
he’d written:

“Everyone loves a Jesus.
Nobody wants to be one.”

She recognized the tone.

Not a slogan.
Not a play for sympathy.

Just a man admitting he was crushed
by the gap between what people demanded of him
and what he could actually bear.

She knew that feeling.

She’d grown up with a father
the world expected to save or destroy them,
depending on which channel they watched.

Her father loved the expectation.
Fed on it.

This man sounded like it made him sick.

She closed the scroll and sat there,
staring at the wall,

realizing that for the first time in her career
she was building a psychological profile on someone

who had never asked for power
and might actually mean it.

She did not report that.

She just wrote, in her private notebook:

Danger level: High.
Not because he wants to rule.
Because he might refuse to.

It was the first actionable insight
she kept for herself.

Not because she planned to use it.

But because something in her,
very quietly,

hoped she might one day need to protect him
from the machine
she was still helping to run.

⸻

This is the record of the moment
Sandi Carmichael stopped seeing One
as a file

and started seeing him
as the variable

her father’s system
was not designed to survive.
