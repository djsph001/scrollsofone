Scene: The Two Wings

A week later, the meeting wasn’t a rally.

It was a hallway.

Miguel called it “the real Capitol.”
One called it “the hinge.”

They walked into a committee office with old carpet and stale coffee. A staffer with tired eyes greeted them like she’d seen a thousand crusades die in binders.

“What is this regarding?” she asked.

Miguel handed her a folder.

“Haiti Corridor transparency compliance.”

The staffer blinked.
“Are you with a party?”

One answered before Miguel could.

“No,” he said. “We’re with deliverables.”

The staffer gave a short skeptical smile.
“Okay. Everybody says that.”

One tapped the folder.

“Then here’s the difference,” he said. “Receipts.”

Inside were three things:
	1.	the MOU with clauses highlighted
	2.	screenshots of missing portal features
	3.	a one-page correction plan with deadlines

The staffer flipped through.

“This is… organized,” she said.

“Because the goal isn’t outrage,” Miguel said. “The goal is compliance.”

She looked up at One.

“Why are you here?” she asked.

One didn’t perform. He spoke like someone who understood America.

“Because the country is divided,” he said. “And divided countries change in two ways: collapse—or boring agreements enforced by boring offices.”

The staffer stared.

“That’s not what people say on TV.”

“That’s why TV doesn’t change policy,” One replied.

She almost laughed. Almost.

Then professionalism.

“You want what?”

“Three corrections,” One said. “Export enabled. Vendor details published. Local percentage shown. Ten business days.”

“That’s aggressive,” she said.

“It’s timely,” One replied. “Haiti doesn’t live on legislative calendars.”

“And if it doesn’t happen?” she asked.

Miguel answered.
“Formal complaints, hearings, donor-side transparency conditions—and a published gap report.”

“You’re threatening,” she said.

One shook his head.
“We’re forecasting.”

Two days later, they met the other wing.

Not party.

Mechanism.

An Inspector General liaison—career civil servant, immune to slogans.

He didn’t care about speeches.
He cared about paper.

“This is clean,” he said. “Your citations match the agreement.”

Miguel nodded.
“We’re not guessing. We’re comparing promises to implementation.”

The liaison laid out the leverage like a map:
	•	request internal procurement docs
	•	demand export justification
	•	require vendor transparency compliance
	•	set inspection timeline

Miguel listened like he’d found the gears.

One listened like his doctrine had become real.

⸻

That night, One met Helen in the library back room.

“You’re lobbying,” Helen said. “You okay with that?”

“Yes,” One replied. “Because this is what people misunderstand.”

He looked tired but steady.

“When you don’t have power,” he said, “you don’t get to be romantic. You get to be precise.”

“And the people who think it’s caving?” Helen asked.

“Let them think it,” One said. “I’m using both wings because that’s how lasting change happens in a divided country.”

He paused.

“I’m not in office,” he said. “I’m not a representative. I’m leverage. Timing. Paper.”

Helen nodded.
“And when influence becomes power?”

One looked at the folder—clauses, deadlines, receipts.

“Then we stop requesting transparency,” he said. “And we make it standard.”
