Scene: The Haitian Network

They didn’t call it a network.

Networks sound like strategy.
This was older than strategy.

It was kinship.

The woman who opened the door—Auntie Jo—wasn’t an aunt by blood. She was an aunt by history. A woman who had cooked for three different families and raised half the block with discipline and rice.

She looked at Sandi like she was both fragile and stubborn.

“Sit,” she said. “Eat.”

Sandi tried to refuse. “I’m okay—”

Auntie Jo’s eyes narrowed.

“You’re carrying life,” she said. “You don’t argue with soup.”

One almost smiled, but didn’t.

He understood immediately what this was:

Not charity.

Protection with dignity.

They kept the radio on low—not for news, for noise. A cheap Haitian station with a DJ who talked too much and played Kompa like it was medicine.

Neighbors came in and out without looking curious. That was the first rule.

Curiosity is an invitation.
Respect is a wall.

Auntie Jo’s nephew—tall, quiet—installed an old router and set up a local-only drive share so they could store copies without cloud exposure.

No speeches. No “I’m honored.”
Just work.

A cousin brought groceries at odd hours.
A friend of a friend drove them once, no questions.
A barber down the street offered the back room if they ever needed a temporary hide.

None of it was announced. None of it was posted.

And that’s why it worked.

Outside, the slur #HaitianBlood kept being flung around online like a dirty coin.

Inside this apartment, Haitian blood meant something else:

It meant people who had survived governments that “disappeared” men.

People who didn’t need to be convinced that proof could be edited, or that officials could lie with perfect confidence.

Auntie Jo sat at the table one night while One labeled a drive.

She watched him for a long time, then spoke.

“You know why they hate you?” she asked.

One didn’t look up. “Because I won’t perform.”

Auntie Jo nodded once.

“Yes,” she said. “And because you remind them that poor people can see.”

One paused.

Sandi sat quietly, listening.

Auntie Jo continued, voice steady, not dramatic.

“In Haiti, we learned early,” she said. “The man on the radio is not the man in the street. The man on the TV is not the man who knocked at your door.”

She pointed gently at the hard drives.

“So you do right,” she said. “You keep the originals.”

Sandi swallowed.

“My father will say they’re hiding me,” she said quietly.

Auntie Jo snorted.

“Let him talk,” she said. “Talking is how cowards fight.”

One looked up, surprised.

Auntie Jo met his eyes.

“You want to protect the baby?” she said.

Sandi’s hand went to her belly.

Auntie Jo nodded.

“Then you keep your circle small,” she said. “And you keep your truth clean.”

One exhaled slowly.

The regime had money.

It had cameras.

It had platforms.

But it didn’t have this:

A community that didn’t need likes to stay loyal.

That night, a message came through One’s burner phone—one of the librarian contacts.

Decoy room pushed hard. People confused. Need a calm method response.

One read it.

Sandi looked at him.

“What,” she asked.

One’s voice stayed steady.

“They built a fake room,” he said. “To make verification look like a cult.”

Auntie Jo laughed, sharp.

“Of course,” she said. “That’s what they do.”

Sandi’s eyes hardened.

“We respond with a checklist,” she said.

One nodded.

Auntie Jo stood.

She placed a bowl of soup in front of Sandi like it was a decree.

“And you respond,” she said, “after you eat.”

Sandi almost smiled.

One felt something shift in his chest.

Not hope exactly.

Something sturdier:

A reminder that the machine was not the whole world.

The world also contained kitchens.

Hands.

Soup.

People who understood that restraint is not weakness—
it is a kind of strength that doesn’t need permission.
