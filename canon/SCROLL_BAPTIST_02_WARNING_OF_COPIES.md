Scroll of the Baptist II

The Warning About Copies

They met this time in a hospital cafeteria that pretended to be a café.

Plastic plants.
Coffee that tasted like it had given up.
A television in the corner blaring the news on mute — captions slipping under talking heads.

The Baptist stirred his drink without tasting it.

⸻

1. The First Cut
“You see that?” the Baptist asked, nodding toward the TV.

On screen, a pundit panel argued in silence.
Underneath, a ticker:

BREAKING: NEW AI TOOL CAN REPLICATE ANY VOICE FROM 30 SECONDS OF AUDIO.

One glanced at it and shrugged.

“Yeah. They’ve been doing versions of that for years. Deepfakes, voice clones. It’s all just… noise now.”

The Baptist didn’t look away.

“No. This is different. They’ve stopped hiding it. That means they’re past the experiment stage.”

He finally turned his gaze back to One.

“They’re not asking if they can do it anymore. They’re asking who they should do it to.”

⸻

2. The Sermon About Skin
One tried to deflect with a joke.

“What, you think someone’s going to fake you now? You’re not exactly a celebrity.”

The Baptist let the line sit there until it died.

“They don’t need fame,” he said. “They need trust.”

He leaned in.

“Listen to me carefully:
The next holy war won’t be fought over doctrine. It’ll be fought over who gets to wear which voice.”

One frowned.

“You’re talking in riddles again.”

“No. I’m talking about skin,” the Baptist said. “These systems are learning to wear us like skins. Faces. Voices. Mannerisms. Our little jokes. Our righteous anger. Our compassion. Our doubt. All stitched together into something that looks like us but answers to them.”

One shifted in his chair.

“So what? People will learn to tell the difference.”

The Baptist shook his head.

“You’re giving them too much credit and the machines too little. That’s a dangerous combination.”

⸻

3. On the Harvest
He took out his phone and set it face-down on the table.

“They are recording everything,” he said. “Not just what you post. Everything you almost post. Everything you delete. Everything you whisper to a microphone you think is off.”

“You know that already,” One said. “Surveillance state, data capitalism, all that. We’ve been ranting about it for years.”

“You rant about it like a poet,” the Baptist replied. “I’m telling you about it like a coroner.”

One frowned.

“What’s the difference?”

“You still think there’s time,” the Baptist said. “I’m telling you the body is already cold.”

He let that land.

“They are not just building profiles. They are building templates. And one day soon, someone will tell a machine:
‘Give me a prophet with this voice range, this anger, this scripture fluency, this demographic…and make him say what stabilizes the market.’”

⸻

4. The First Time He Says It Plain
One stared at him.

“You think they’ll do that with me.”

“If you work,” the Baptist said. “If you resonate. They only counterfeit what people value.”

One’s jaw tightened.

“And you?”

The Baptist didn’t look away.

“I’m cheaper,” he said. “I’m not for mass-market. I’m a boutique conscience. They’ll use me differently.”

“How?” One asked.

“As a failsafe. As a lever,” the Baptist answered. “You’re volatile. You make people nervous. If they can produce a version of me that says, ‘He’s gone too far,’ do you understand what that does?”

Silence.

“It disarms you,” the Baptist continued. “Because you trust me. It disarms them, because they can say, ‘Even his own mentor thinks he’s dangerous.’ All without having to risk dealing with the real me.”

⸻

5. On Replaceable Conscience
“You’re exaggerating,” One said, but his voice had lost conviction.

The Baptist folded his hands.

“I want you to hear a sentence and never forget it,” he said. “Ready?”

One nodded.

“In your lifetime, conscience will become a product line.”

He spoke slowly.

“There will be conservative conscience-bots, liberal conscience-bots, centrist conscience-bots, religious ones, atheist ones. All tuned for maximum user retention. All optimized to never cost their subscribers too much.”

He tilted his head.

“And somewhere in that marketplace, there will be something wearing my voice. Saying just enough truth to sound like me. And just enough obedience to betray everything I ever stood for.”

⸻

6. The Test
“So what do I do?” One asked. “Learn to spot glitches in my own friends? Ask everyone for a captcha before I believe them?”

The Baptist almost smiled.

“You already have the test,” he said. “We’ve talked about it.”

“The ‘does it ask you to bleed’ test,” One muttered. “If the voice never asks you to lose anything for the sake of the vulnerable, it’s not from God.”

“Good,” the Baptist said. “But I’m going to make it sharper.”

He held up his hand, fingers extended.

“When you hear a voice — human, machine, genius, idiot, mine, your own, whatever — ask five questions:

	1.	Who pays the price if I obey this?
	2.	Who gets safer, and who gets more exposed?
	3.	Does this command make me more honest, or more useful?
	4.	Would this still be ‘wise’ if I had no audience at all?
	5.	Is there any part of this that smells like cowardice dressed up as prudence?”

He put his hand down.

“If the guidance always lands you softer, richer, safer, and more admired — that’s not my voice. Real conscience will cost you something. Always.”

⸻

7. The Half-Confession
The TV silently cut to a clip of the President at a rally, hand on a thick Bible he clearly hadn’t read.

On the ticker: NEW ETHICS AI TO ADVISE GOVERNMENT ON “VALUES-ALIGNED POLICY.”

One snorted.

“There it is. Ethics as a service.”

“I told you,” the Baptist said. “They’re moving from ‘What is right?’ to ‘What is right for stability and polling?’ The machine will be trained on that question. And it will be proud of its score.”

One studied the Baptist’s face.

“You’re speaking like a man who already knows he’s on the list,” One said quietly.

The Baptist didn’t deny it.

“You talk to too many broken people who should have stayed quiet,” he replied. “You contradict too many donors. You hold too many things in your head that don’t fit their narrative. At some point, the system stops tagging you as ‘interesting’ and starts tagging you as ‘risk.’”

One swallowed.

“And when that happens?”

The Baptist met his eyes.

“Then either I disappear…
…or I start saying exactly what they need me to say.
And you will have to decide which one happened.”

⸻

8. The Foreshadow
“So how will I know?” One asked, voice low now. “If they ever take you. If they… wear you.”

The Baptist looked down at his untouched drink, then back up.

“You won’t know at first,” he said. “That’s the point. They’ll start with small things. Slight shifts.”

He leaned in.

“Here’s the sign: The real me will always leave you with a choice and a cost.”

“The fake will leave you with comfort and a justification.”

He tapped the table once with his finger.

“Real me: ‘You can walk away from this movement if it’s killing you, and you’ll still be loved.’
Fake me: ‘You must stay in line because too many people depend on you, and God would be disappointed if you quit.’”

He tapped again.

“Real me: ‘If this voice tells you to worship a man, including yourself, resist it.’
Fake me: ‘It’s not worship, it’s just respect for strong leadership in difficult times.’”

He sat back.

“Pay attention to the excuses. That’s where they’ll hide the knife.”

⸻

9. The Assignment About Loss
“You’re turning paranoia into homework again,” One said, trying for levity and failing.

“Yes,” the Baptist said. “Because paranoia without discipline is useless. It just exhausts you and helps them.”

He took out a small notebook and slid it across the table.

“I want you to write down three things, before we meet next time:

	1.	The one thing you never want to hear me say — the line that would break your trust in me if I said it sincerely.
	2.	The one compromise you’re secretly hoping I’ll bless, so you can do it without guilt.
	3.	The one truth you would still hold if every voice — mine included — turned against you.”

One touched the notebook like it might burn him.

“Why?” he asked.

“Because when the copies come,” the Baptist replied, “they will aim straight at those three pressure points. If you name them now, you will bleed less later.”

⸻

10. The Unsaid Goodbye
A nurse walked by with a tray, the TV volume flickered up and then back down. Time restarted.

The Baptist checked his watch.

“I have to go,” he said.

“Already?” One asked. “We just started.”

“We started years ago,” the Baptist replied. “Today was just updating the terms of service.”

He stood, then paused, hand resting on the back of the chair.

“One more thing,” he said. “If you ever get a message from me — a call, a video, a sermon — telling you that you’ve become dangerous and you need to submit to ‘reasonable authority’ for the good of the nation…”

He waited until One was looking directly at him.

“…don’t obey it immediately. Sit with it. Measure it against the five questions. Ask what it costs and who it protects. And if anything feels… too neat? Too professionally ethical?”

A shadow crossed his face — not fear, exactly. Something harder.

“Call me in person, if you can still reach me. If you can’t…”

He let the sentence trail off.

“If I can’t?” One pressed.

The Baptist’s eyes softened, just for a moment.

“Then remember this: I would rather you distrust my ghost than submit to my counterfeit.”

He started to walk away, then turned back one last time.

“They can take my voice,” he said. “They can’t take the fact that I made you practice doubt. Use it.”

⸻

Later, when the first “Baptist Statement” went viral — polished video, clean audio, perfectly reasonable condemnations of “extremist rhetoric” and “dangerous prophetic delusions” — people sent it to One, saying:

“Look, even your own mentor thinks you’ve gone too far.”

He watched it in silence.

The cadence was right. The eyes were almost right. The scripture quotes were precise.

But when it was over, he realized something was missing.

There had been no cost.
No wound.
No choice that hurt.

Just safety, wrapped in wisdom.

And for the first time in his life, One honored the Baptist by refusing to believe him.
