Scroll of the Baptist I: First Counsel

He didn’t arrive like a miracle.
He arrived like a man who had been waiting a long time and was tired of watching people pretend they weren’t afraid.

One met him in a small place that didn’t look important enough to hold history—an after-hours church basement in Brooklyn, a folding table, bad coffee, and a room that smelled like dust and old hymn books.

One didn’t introduce himself as a prophet.

He introduced himself as a problem.

“I can’t unsee it,” One said. “The lies. The performance. The way people turn cruelty into policy and call it order.”

The Baptist watched him for a long moment—not impressed, not skeptical, just… measuring.

Then he asked the first question that mattered:

“Do you want to be right, or do you want to be clean?”

One frowned. “What does that mean?”

“It means,” the Baptist said, “you can fight evil and still become a weapon. You can oppose tyranny and still learn its habits. You can tell the truth and still poison it with ego.”

One looked down at his hands like he was checking whether they were already stained.

The Baptist leaned forward.

“Listen carefully. You don’t need followers. You need practices.”

One bristled. “I’m not starting a cult.”

“Good,” the Baptist said. “Then don’t act like a man who needs applause to breathe.”

Silence landed heavy.

Outside, sirens. A bus hissed at the curb. Brooklyn kept moving like nothing holy had ever happened.

The Baptist spoke again, softer.

“They’re going to try to make you a symbol. The regime will do it to destroy you. Your supporters will do it to worship you. Both are traps.”

“What do I do?” One asked.

The Baptist held up one finger.

“First: Refuse the stage.
Never accept a debate that requires you to perform their script. Never answer a question designed to turn you into a headline.”

A second finger.

“Second: Build a rule the crowd can’t break.
If your movement depends on your charisma, you’ve already lost.”

A third finger.

“And third—this is the one people hate—verify everything.
Not because you’re paranoid. Because the age you’re entering will sell people a version of you that feels more convenient than the real thing.”

One stared at him. “A version of me?”

The Baptist nodded once, as if naming something obvious.

“Voices can be copied now. Faces can be worn. Screens can preach with your mouth and ruin you with your tone.”

One tried to laugh it off, but it came out thin.

The Baptist didn’t move.

“You think I’m being dramatic,” he said. “I’m not. I’m warning you with mercy.”

One swallowed.

“What are you, exactly?” he asked.

The Baptist’s eyes held him steady.

“A witness,” he said. “And for a while—if you’ll accept it—your brake.”

One leaned back, exhausted already.

“And if I refuse?” One asked.

The Baptist looked past him, toward the door, toward the world.

“Then you’ll still become famous,” he said. “And you’ll still get people hurt. And you’ll still meet your enemy. The only question is whether you’ll arrive there as a man… or as a product.”

One didn’t answer.

The Baptist stood up, picked up his coat, and left him with one final line—quiet, almost kind:

“Remember Elijah: not the wind, not the earthquake, not the fire. The still small voice.
Protect it. That voice is how you stay human.”

Scroll of Conscience III: Conscious Responsibility

Conscience is not a feeling.
It is a duty.

It is the moment you realize your choices echo beyond your mood, beyond your tribe, beyond the room you’re in—and you choose anyway, with your eyes open.

Conscious Responsibility means:
	•	You do not outsource your moral judgment to a crowd.
	•	You do not call harm “necessary” just because it’s efficient.
	•	You do not trade a human being for a strategy.
	•	You do not summon catastrophe and claim it is faith.

In an age of machines, the temptation is this:

To become lazy in the soul.

To say: the algorithm decided.
To say: the market demanded.
To say: the party required.
To say: I was only following the feed.

No.

You are still accountable.

And the proof of conscience is not what you believe.
It is what you refuse to do.

If technology grows powerful, then responsibility must grow faster.

If your voice reaches millions, then restraint must become your literacy.

If you can persuade a crowd, then you must ask:
Am I telling the truth… or am I bending reality to win?

For it is written:
“By their fruits you shall know them.”

Not by their charisma.
Not by their certainty.
Not by their enemies.

By their fruits.

So here is the civic ethic:
	1.	Tell the truth you can verify.
	2.	Name what you don’t know.
	3.	Do not weaponize the innocent.
	4.	Do not romanticize collapse.
	5.	Do not build systems that require cruelty to function.

Conscious Responsibility is how a person stays human inside an empire of spectacle.

It is how a movement stays clean inside a market of manipulation.

And it is how a future stays possible.
