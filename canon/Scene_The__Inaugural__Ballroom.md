Scene: The Inaugural Ballroom

The ballroom was Carmichael’s idea of mercy.

Not mercy as in forgiveness—mercy as in beauty applied like pressure.

Gold light poured from chandeliers that looked too new to be honest. The ceiling was a cathedral pretending it wasn’t. The floor—dark polished wood—reflected everyone back at themselves in soft, flattering distortion. Even the air smelled curated: citrus, expensive cologne, and something clean that didn’t exist outside controlled spaces.

A string orchestra warmed up in the corner, tuning their instruments like the room itself needed alignment.

At the far end, beneath a drape of flags and floral arrangements, President Carmichael stood smiling the way cameras loved him: relaxed, paternal, generous. He was surrounded by donors, senators, and the kind of people who never carried their own coats.

One stood off to the side near a column, holding a drink he didn’t want and wearing a suit that fit well enough to pass, if nobody looked too hard. He’d watched a thousand rooms like this from the edges. He knew how power moved in circles—how it fed on admiration without ever admitting hunger.

Tonight, though, he wasn’t watching Carmichael.

He was watching Sandi.

She entered without announcement. No handler. No entourage. No forced smile for the room. She moved like someone who grew up inside these spaces and didn’t confuse them for safety.

She wore a dark dress that didn’t scream for attention, which somehow drew it. Her hair was pulled back simply, like she wasn’t here to be admired—she was here because she had to be.

And when she paused at the threshold, scanning the room once, One noticed the tiniest thing: her eyes didn’t search for the spotlight.

They searched for exits.

He felt a familiar jolt—half curiosity, half ache.

And then, beneath it: intention.

Tonight.
Ask her tonight.

One adjusted his cuff, a meaningless gesture meant to anchor his hands. He’d practiced the steps in his head all week like a prayer: the frame, the timing, the pivot. He’d grown up dancing long before he learned the language of policy, and the body—when trained—remembered truth faster than the mouth did.

He had told himself it was just a moment of levity. A small human thing inside a machine.

But he knew the real reason.

He wanted to see if she could feel him as something other than a name in her father’s universe.

Across the room, Carmichael lifted a glass and began speaking. The crowd quieted instantly, like obedience had been installed in the architecture.

“Tonight,” the President said, voice warm and practiced, “we open this ballroom not only as a space of celebration, but as a symbol of unity—of the American spirit, of tradition, of renewal.”

Applause. Perfectly timed.

Sandi stood near the side, clapping politely, not looking at him.

One watched her face during the speech. She didn’t soften. She didn’t glow. She didn’t collapse into the story the room wanted her to embody.

She looked… tired.

Not physically. Soul-tired.

The speech ended. The orchestra shifted into something familiar and inviting. The crowd loosened into movement—small clusters drifting toward the dance floor, laughter returning like a rehearsed cue.

One’s pulse rose.

He took a breath, then started walking.

Each step felt like crossing a border.

Sandi was near a table of champagne flutes, speaking to an older woman who kept touching her arm the way society women touched you when they were really touching your reputation. Sandi nodded, smiled once, and then—like a magician—slid out of the conversation without creating offense.

She turned and almost bumped into One.

He stopped short, shoulders squared, hands visible and calm—like he was approaching a skittish animal and didn’t want to spook it.

For a beat, they just looked at each other.

Sandi’s eyes moved over him quickly: suit, posture, the deliberate steadiness. She didn’t smile yet.

“Hi,” she said, neutral.

One swallowed. “Hi.”

She waited, giving him space to be either a fan, a political climber, or something else.

One chose something else.

“This place is…” He glanced up at the chandeliers. “Hard to ignore.”

Sandi’s mouth twitched. Almost a smile.

“That’s the point,” she said. “If it’s too beautiful, people stop asking what it cost.”

That landed in him like a bell.

“You noticed,” he said.

She gave him a look that was almost amused. “I notice everything. It’s a survival skill.”

One nodded, and for a second his face softened. “Same.”

Sandi’s gaze sharpened slightly. “Do I know you?”

“Not really,” he said. “I’m… around.”

Around could mean anything in this town: staff, advisor, witness, ghost.

Sandi glanced toward her father across the room, then back. “That’s vague.”

“It’s honest,” One said, and the words surprised even him as they left his mouth.

Sandi studied him for another beat, as if deciding whether she liked that answer.

The orchestra shifted into a waltz—clean, elegant, unavoidable.

One felt his moment arrive like a wave.

He cleared his throat once, gentle.

“Would you like to dance?”

Sandi blinked. The question was so normal it almost didn’t belong in the room. It made her look at him again, properly this time.

“You’re asking me,” she said, “to dance. Here.”

“Yes,” One said. Then, before he could stop himself, he added with a faint, self-aware smile: “I was hoping to impress you.”

Sandi’s eyebrows rose. “With what?”

“With my ballroom dancing skills,” he said, and for the first time his voice carried a thread of boyish confidence.

Sandi laughed—quiet, surprised. “You’re serious.”

“One hundred percent,” he said.

She tilted her head. “Why?”

One could’ve answered with something clean and political. Something safe.

Instead he told her the truth he could afford.

“Because you look like you’d rather be anywhere else,” he said. “And dancing is… one of the few things in a room like this that can still be real.”

Sandi’s face softened, and then—like a door briefly opening—she let something personal show.

“I’m recently divorced,” she said, almost casually, like dropping a fact on the table to see if it changes the conversation. “I have two kids.”

One didn’t react with pity. He didn’t flinch.

He just nodded like that mattered and didn’t scare him.

“Okay,” he said.

Sandi’s eyes narrowed, amused. “That’s it?”

“That’s not nothing,” One said. “That’s a whole universe.”

Sandi looked at him longer now.

Then she glanced toward the dance floor where couples were already turning in neat circles, smiling for cameras, for donors, for the night.

“You know,” she said, “people don’t usually ask me to dance. They ask me to endorse their existence.”

One held her gaze. “I’m asking you to dance.”

Sandi considered him.

Then she nodded once. “Fine.”

One offered his hand. Not too eager. Not too formal. A simple invitation.

Sandi placed her hand in his, and for a second One felt the shock of it—warmth, pressure, real contact in a room built on performance.

He led her to the floor.

The first few measures passed as he positioned them. Frame. Distance. Respect.

Sandi’s posture was good—better than she expected, which told One she’d danced before. Or had been trained to.

“You’ve done this,” he said softly.

Sandi smiled slightly. “I’ve done the versions that aren’t about joy.”

One nodded, like he understood more than he could say.

He guided her into the waltz.

And it worked.

Not perfectly—nothing with two real people ever is—but cleanly enough that Sandi’s shoulders dropped. The tension eased out of her frame. The performance receded.

For the first time that night, she looked like she could breathe.

“You’re actually good,” she said.

One let himself smile. “Did I tell you I was a great dancer?”

Sandi laughed, genuinely now. “You did. But you didn’t tell me you were brave.”

“I’m not brave,” One said. “I’m just… stubborn about certain truths.”

Sandi watched him. “Like what?”

He turned her gently, letting the music carry the question for a beat.

“Like the truth that you’re not here because you want to be,” he said.

Sandi’s smile faded slightly. Her eyes flicked around the room—cameras, officials, her father’s orbit.

Then back to him.

“You see too much,” she said.

One didn’t deny it. “So do you.”

They moved together, one full rotation, then another.

The room blurred into soft gold behind her. For a few seconds, the ballroom stopped being Carmichael’s monument and became what it pretended to be: a place where two people could exist without being used.

Sandi’s voice lowered. “My kids are with my sister tonight,” she said. “I almost didn’t come.”

One’s hand at her back stayed steady, respectful.

“Why did you?” he asked.

Sandi’s gaze went past his shoulder, toward her father laughing with a senator.

“Because if I don’t show up,” she said quietly, “someone else tells the story for me.”

That sentence—simple, exhausted—hit One like prophecy.

He swallowed, and his voice softened.

“No one should have to live like that,” he said.

Sandi looked up at him. “And you think you can change it?”

One hesitated. The honest answer was complicated.

So he gave her the only truth he trusted.

“I don’t know,” he said. “But I know I can refuse to lie about it.”

Sandi’s eyes held his.

For a moment, she looked like she might say something dangerous—something real.

Instead, she asked the smallest question that mattered.

“What’s your name?” she said.

One smiled, gentle.

“People call me One,” he said.

Sandi’s mouth curved, skeptical. “That’s not a name. That’s branding.”

One’s smile didn’t break. “It’s what stuck.”

She studied him, then nodded once, as if filing him away as a problem she might willingly keep.

“Okay,” she said, “One.”

The music swelled toward its end. The last turn approached like a closing door.

As the final note landed, One eased them to a stop.

Sandi didn’t pull away immediately.

And then—right on cue—the room intruded again.

A photographer moved closer. A staffer angled for a shot. Carmichael’s orbit shifted subtly, like the machine had noticed something human happen without permission.

Sandi’s face tightened slightly as she saw the attention.

One felt it too.

The old reality returning.

He leaned in just enough for her to hear him over the applause.

“If you ever want to dance again,” he said quietly, “somewhere that isn’t built as a weapon… I’d like that.”

Sandi looked at him. The warmth didn’t leave her eyes, but caution re-entered.

“Where?” she asked.

“One place at a time,” he said.

Sandi’s lips curved again—half laugh, half warning.

“Careful,” she said. “People around here don’t like private joy.”

One nodded, expression calm.

“Then they’ll hate me,” he said softly, “because I’m going to keep choosing it anyway.”

Sandi held his gaze for one more heartbeat.

Then she stepped back, smoothing her dress like putting armor back on.

“Goodnight, One,” she said.

“Goodnight, Sandi,” he replied.

She walked away toward the edge of the room—toward her obligations, her history, her father’s gravity.

One stayed on the dance floor for a moment longer, watching her go, feeling the strange certainty settle into his chest:

This wasn’t just attraction.

This was a collision.

And the ballroom—Carmichael’s shining monument—had just hosted the first crack in its own story.

Because two people had danced like they were real.
