Scene: The Dagger Point

Night had settled into the apartment like dust—quiet, stubborn, everywhere.

The baby finally slept. The kind of sleep that looks like peace until you remember how hard it was earned.

Sandi stood at the sink rinsing a bottle, sleeves rolled up, hair loose, moving with that practiced calm she’d learned since leaving the center of power: calm as a discipline, not a mood.

One sat at the table with his laptop closed for once. The exhibits were there. The timeline. The clean folders. All the things that proved he was right and didn’t make anything safer.

He watched Sandi’s hands.

He watched the small tremor she didn’t know she had.

And something in him cracked—not loud, not theatrical. Just… a break in the seam.

“Sandi,” he said.

She glanced back. “Yeah?”

One opened his mouth, closed it, then tried again—like truth had become a heavy object and his hands were tired.

“I keep thinking…” he began, then stopped, because the sentence was too sharp to hold.

Sandi turned the water off and faced him. “Say it.”

One swallowed. His voice came out low.

“What did I get you into?”

Sandi didn’t answer right away. She just looked at him—steady, waiting, letting him say the whole thing.

He tried to keep it contained, but guilt doesn’t stay where you put it.

“I was supposed to be the one who could take the heat,” he said. “I was supposed to be the one who could be called names, smeared, threatened. Fine. That’s the price of speaking.”

His hands curled slightly on the edge of the table.

“But you…” His throat tightened. “You didn’t ask for this.”

Sandi’s eyes softened, but she didn’t rescue him from it. She let him feel it. That was love too.

“One,” she said gently.

He shook his head, like he couldn’t let himself off the hook.

“No. Listen. They’re using you. They’re using us. They’re taking your face and turning it into a lever. They’re taking—” his voice broke, smaller now— “they’re taking the baby and turning the baby into paperwork.”

He stared down at the table, like he couldn’t bear to look at her while admitting the ugliest part.

“I keep seeing your life before me,” he said. “I keep seeing you as… a daughter who could’ve stayed comfortable. You could’ve lived with distance. You could’ve lived with denial.”

He laughed once, bitter and quiet. “And then I show up with my conscience and my documents and my stupid refusal to lie—”

He finally looked up. His eyes were wet but not pleading.

“And now you’re hunted.”

The silence that followed wasn’t empty. It was full of everything he was trying not to say: I love you. I’m sorry. I would undo it. I can’t undo it.

Sandi walked over slowly and sat across from him.

She reached for his hands and held them—not to calm him, but to keep him present.

“You didn’t drag me,” she said.

One flinched, like he wanted to argue.

Sandi tightened her grip, not hard—just certain.

“Don’t take my choice away,” she said. “That’s what he does. That’s what the system does. It turns women into objects in a story.”

One exhaled, shakily.

“I would’ve stayed a daughter in a cage,” she continued. “A beautiful cage, but a cage.”

She leaned in, voice soft but firm.

“You didn’t pull me into danger,” she said. “You showed me the truth of the danger I was already living in.”

One’s eyes closed for a second. A tear made it out anyway.

“But I hate it,” he whispered. “I hate that loving me costs you.”

Sandi nodded, because she wasn’t going to lie to make him feel better.

“I hate it too,” she said. “But I’d rather pay the honest price than live on the discount lie.”

One looked at her like that sentence hit the deepest part of him.

“And the baby?” he asked, barely audible. “What if—”

Sandi stood, walked to the back room, and returned with the baby tucked against her chest. The child’s head rested under her chin, warm and real.

She held the baby out toward him.

“Then we do what we’ve been doing,” she said. “We keep this real. We keep it small. We keep it protected. And we don’t let them make our love into their theater.”

One took the baby carefully, like holding something sacred and fragile and heavier than law.

He pressed his lips to the child’s hair.

“I’m sorry,” he whispered—not to the baby alone, but to Sandi, to life, to God, to whatever kept watching without intervening.

Sandi sat beside him now, shoulder touching his.

“One,” she said, voice almost a lullaby, “you can regret the pain without regretting the truth.”

He nodded, staring into the quiet.

Then, the smallest hint of steel returned—his true shape.

“I won’t lie,” he said.

Sandi’s hand rested on his arm.

“I know,” she said. “That’s why I’m here.”

And for a moment the machine felt far away—not gone, not defeated—just held at the door by something stronger than fear:

two people refusing to perform.
